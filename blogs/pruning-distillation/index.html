TinyPath is a 103.6M-parameter H&E tile encoder cut from the 1.13B-parameter Prov-GigaPath (Xu et al., 2024) and distilled from it for one epoch. It keeps 98.6% of its teacher’s HEST score, matches its EVA classification average and is far more robust to scanner and stain changes.

H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) and GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026) distill their teachers into standard small architectures. TinyPath instead follows Minitron (Muralidharan et al., 2024), starting from the teacher and keeping only its most important channels, heads, MLP hidden dimensions and blocks. Like its teacher, it is released under Apache-2.0, so it can be used commercially and its derivatives shared. H0-mini’s CC BY-NC-ND 4.0 license allows neither.

Summary

  1. TinyPath keeps nearly all of its teacher’s accuracy and is far more robust. It scores 0.382 against Prov-GigaPath’s 0.387 on HEST, 0.8525 against 0.8524 averaged over five EVA leaderboard classification tasks, and 0.530 against 0.333 on PLISM. PLISM’s code now resizes tiles first, a setting with no published Prov-GigaPath score, so we ran Prov-GigaPath ourselves. There TinyPath scores 0.520 against 0.320.
  2. H0-mini scores higher on HEST but keeps less of its teacher’s score. Distilled from the stronger H-optimus-0 on the same slides, with 2.7 times as many tiles, the 86M-parameter model scores 0.396, 95.4% of its teacher’s, against TinyPath’s 98.6%.
  3. GigaPath-Flash keeps less of the same teacher’s scores. Prov-GigaPath’s own 22M-parameter student, a fifth of TinyPath’s size, has no published scores here. In our runs it keeps 92.7% of the teacher’s HEST score, 96.7% of its EVA patch-task mean and 88.3% of its PLISM score.
  4. TinyPath took about 835 GPU-hours to build. Pruning, including a search over 861 candidate shapes, took 44 GPU-hours. Distillation took 99 hours on eight A100s, one epoch over 78.9 million tiles from 6,093 TCGA slides. The sliced network starts at 0.236 on HEST and passes the teacher within the first tenth of the epoch (The training run).

Results

We score TinyPath’s frozen features on published benchmarks, with at most a light head on top. HEST tests prediction of spatial gene expression (Jaume et al., 2024), EVA patch and slide classification and nucleus segmentation (kaiko.ai et al., 2024), and PLISM robustness to rescanning and restaining (Ochi et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025). On whole slides we run PORPOISE survival (Chen et al., 2022) and the Prov-GigaPath paper’s subtyping and mutation tasks, rebuilt on public TCGA slides (Weinstein et al., 2013). We also compare H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025), an 86M-parameter ViT-B distilled from H-optimus-0 (Saillard et al., 2024), and GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026), a 22M-parameter ViT-S distilled from Prov-GigaPath. Other models’ scores are published where the protocol matches ours. Otherwise we ran them ourselves: Prov-GigaPath under PLISM’s current resize and on slide-level tasks, H0-mini under EVA’s current settings and GigaPath-Flash everywhere (Appendix A, Appendix B).

Tile-level benchmarks

TinyPath keeps 98.6% of Prov-GigaPath’s HEST score. On EVA it averages 0.8525 against 0.8524 over BreakHis, CRC, Gleason, PatchCamelyon and Camelyon16-small, the classification tasks in EVA’s leaderboard average that we ran. Over the five patch tasks in Figure 1, which swap Camelyon16-small for BACH, it averages 0.837 against 0.841. On PLISM it scores about 0.2 higher.

TinyPath’s losses are concentrated in a few tasks (Figure 2). On HEST, colon and rectum account for more than the whole deficit. On EVA it trails only on BreakHis, the noisiest task, which swung by up to 0.07 between checkpoints, and on MoNuSAC, the only benchmark here that reads patch tokens. Its largest lead, on Camelyon16-small, is about one standard deviation.

The PLISM gain holds under the current resize and is mostly retrieval: TinyPath is far more likely to find the same tissue under another scanner or stain (Figure 3). H0-mini also beats its teacher, by less (0.541 against 0.480), but GigaPath-Flash, distilled on Providence slides, scores 0.294 in our run against its teacher’s 0.333 (Table 5). The two students that gain were both distilled on the same 6,093 TCGA slides, which may smooth away acquisition-specific detail.

Slide-level tasks

TinyPath trained on slides from 443 of the 444 patients in our PORPOISE LUAD cohort and on 1,438 of the 1,503 slides behind our Prov-GigaPath tasks, though not on their labels. Prov-GigaPath saw none of them, so these results describe the representation rather than measure generalization.

No published Prov-GigaPath score matches these protocols. PORPOISE never evaluated it, and its own paper used its LongNet slide encoder on random splits of mostly private slides, so we ran it ourselves. Our PORPOISE rebuild also differs from the paper in cohort, tiling and head seeds, so we reran the paper’s ResNet-50 baseline (He et al., 2016) through it (Appendix A). TinyPath’s subtyping and mutation averages come within 0.01 AUROC of its teacher’s, but it trails by 0.033 on breast ductal-versus-lobular subtyping (Table 8). On LUAD survival, TinyPath, Prov-GigaPath and ResNet-50 land within a little over one standard error of each other and inside the paper’s ResNet-50 interval (Table 7), though histology alone predicts survival weakly.

Two other compact encoders

H0-mini is the strongest compact encoder here on HEST, above Prov-GigaPath itself, but its teacher is stronger too. It keeps 95.4% of H-optimus-0’s score (95.7% under its own protocol), against TinyPath’s 98.6%, and TinyPath keeps the larger share on seven of HEST’s nine tasks. On EVA’s patch tasks TinyPath keeps 99.5% and H0-mini 99.8%, and on PLISM 159% and 113% (Figure 4). The two share training slides but little else: H0-mini is a ViT-B with 14-pixel patches and four register tokens, does not start from its teacher’s weights, distills through pretrained heads that Prov-GigaPath does not ship and saw 2.7 times as many tiles, so retention cannot say which difference matters.

GigaPath-Flash shares TinyPath’s teacher. It is a ViT-S/16 distilled on Providence slides with the DINOv2 recipe minus KoLeo, and its authors report it only through its own LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023), on custom splits of PANDA prostate grading (Bulten et al., 2022) and EBRAINS brain tumor subtyping (Roetzer-Pejrimovsky et al., 2022), where it keeps 96.8% of its teacher’s average. Through our tile-level pipelines it keeps 92.7% of Prov-GigaPath’s HEST score, 96.7% of its EVA patch-task mean and 88.3% of its PLISM score, the only student below its teacher there. TinyPath keeps the larger share on every EVA patch task and on seven of HEST’s nine (Figure 4), though the two also differ in size and training slides.

How TinyPath is built

Pruning reads activations off the frozen teacher, picks the student’s shape and copies the kept weights. Distillation then retrains the pruned network (Figure 5). Minitron was written for language models, so we replaced the parts a ViT lacks, such as vocabulary logits and perplexity (Appendix D).

The teacher is a ViT-g/16 (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024) pretrained on 1.38 billion tiles from 171,189 slides (Xu et al., 2024). It has 40 blocks on a 1,536-channel residual stream, each with 24 attention heads of 64 dimensions and a SwiGLU MLP (Shazeer, 2020) whose hidden size, the width between its two projections, is 4,096. Prov-GigaPath’s separate LongNet slide encoder is left untouched.

Pruning uses 4,640 slides of a 5,802-slide TCGA split made by patient: 580 supply the tiles that score importance, 580 score architecture candidates and 3,480 train each candidate’s short probe, so no candidate is scored on tissue it was calibrated or trained on. The other 1,162 are held out. Distillation trains on all 6,093 TCGA slides H0-mini was distilled on.

Pruning

Pruning scores which structural units carry the signal, searches for how many of each the budget affords and slices the survivors out of the teacher. Each of the four axes is ranked on its own (Figure 6), and the head count is pinned to width divided by 64, keeping attention width equal to residual width as in the teacher.

Scoring what the teacher actually uses

We score activations rather than weights, since a component with large weights that rarely activates matters less than one with small weights that activates on everything. One hooked forward pass over 4,096 calibration tiles scores all four axes in under two minutes on one GPU (Figure 7). For depth, a ViT has no perplexity, so we use Minitron’s other signal, ShortGPT’s block importance (Men et al., 2024; Muralidharan et al., 2024), which scores a block by how far it rotates its input.

The importance scores in detail Eq. 1–3

For an activation \(a_{b,n,c}\) at calibration example \(b\), token \(n\) and component \(c\), the three width axes first collapse the token axis,

\[ r_{b,c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|a_{b,n,c}\right|, \tag{1}\]

and then take an L2 norm across examples:

\[ s_{c}=\sqrt{\sum_{b} r_{b,c}^{2}}. \tag{2}\]

The absolute value stops a component that fires in opposite directions on different tokens from cancelling to zero, and accumulating squares before the root makes the score independent of batching, so the ranking reproduces across machines.

Heads are scored as units: the packed attention output is reshaped into 24 head vectors, each reduced to its L2 norm. MLP hidden dimensions are hooked at the input to the second projection, where the gate and value branches are already combined. In SwiGLU’s packed first projection, hidden dimension \(i\) owns rows \(i\) and \(i+m\), with \(m\) the MLP hidden size, so both rows are kept or cut together. Channels are hooked at both LayerNorms of every block and summed over the trunk, since a residual channel cannot be kept in one block and dropped in another.

Depth is scored by block importance, ShortGPT’s block influence (Men et al., 2024), the mean cosine change across the residual path,

\[ \mathrm{BI}_{i}=1-\mathbb{E}_{b,n}\left[\cos\left(x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{in}},x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)\right], \tag{3}\]

averaged over every token of every calibration tile. Blocks are ranked on \(\mathrm{BI}\) across the whole network, not within a local window.

Choosing a shape, not a sparsity ratio

The budget is 100 million backbone parameters, from 5% over to 15% under, and 861 shapes fit it. Shapes of equal size can differ in representation, so the search slices each by top-\(k\) against the rankings, trains it for a 1,000-step probe and ranks it by CKA against the teacher on held-out tiles (Kornblith et al., 2019). The winner is 640 channels, 33 blocks, 10 heads and an MLP hidden size of 768, at 103,630,848 parameters. The probe takes about five and a half hours on eight GPUs and matters: the winner ranks last of the eventual top five before it and first after.

The search space and the three ranking signals

The grid is every hardware-friendly shape: widths from 64 to 1,536 in steps of 64, depths from 1 to 40 and MLP hidden sizes from 256 to 4,096 in steps of 256, with head width pinned at 64. Of its 15,360 shapes, 861 land between 85 and 105 million parameters. Each candidate is a straight top-\(k\) slice of the teacher: blocks and residual channels from their global rankings, heads and MLP hidden dimensions from their per-block rankings.

The three signals, cheapest first:

  • Subspace cosine. The candidate’s pooled features against the teacher’s on the surviving channels. Nearly free, but only a diagnostic here, since every candidate went on to the probe.
  • Linear CKA. Compares the geometry of two feature sets regardless of width or channel order, so a 640-wide candidate can be scored directly against the 1,536-wide teacher.
  • CKA after a 1,000-step probe. Each candidate trains briefly on the hidden-state losses alone, cycling through 2,048 tiles from the 3,480 probe slides, then CKA is recomputed on 512 tiles from the 580 architecture-validation slides. This is the ranking key, with parameter error as the tiebreak.

The margin at the top is thin: the winner scores 0.7721 and the next two, 768 channels over 25 blocks and 704 over 25, score 0.7717 and 0.7715. Before the probe the same five shapes score between 0.2988 and 0.3095, the winner lowest. After it the winner also has the lowest probe loss of the five, 1.181 against 1.217 to 1.494.

The probe leaves out the DINO and iBOT heads, since a 131,072-way prototype head is still mostly noise after 1,000 steps and would rank candidates by their initialization.

Making the cut

Slicing is bookkeeping: every index must reach every tensor it touches, or the network ends up subtly wrong in a way no shape check will catch. Block importance kept blocks 0–5 and 13–39, so the seven dropped blocks form one band in the early-middle of the trunk (Figure 8). The first and last blocks were not forced to survive.

What each index touches
  • The 640 surviving residual channels index everywhere the residual stream appears: the patch projection, CLS token, positional embedding, every LayerNorm, every block’s input and output, and the final norm.
  • A surviving head takes its rows out of Q, K and V and the matching columns of the attention output projection.
  • A surviving MLP hidden dimension takes its paired gate and value rows out of the packed first projection and the matching column of the second.
  • Surviving blocks keep their own LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) and LayerScale (Touvron et al., 2021) parameters, in the teacher’s original order.

The cut is made in one shot, as Minitron recommends for all but its most aggressive reduction (Muralidharan et al., 2024).

What the cut costs

Before training, the sliced network scores 0.236 on HEST against the teacher’s 0.387, and shown the same tissue on two scanners it puts the match among its ten nearest neighbors 0.2% of the time, against the teacher’s 57% (Figure 9).

PLISM shows what is wrong. Two scans of the same tissue position give the sliced network a median cosine similarity of 0.936, far above the teacher’s 0.553, yet for almost every tile at least ten unrelated positions sit closer than its twin, so the network likely maps most tiles to nearly the same direction. Activation-based importance alone does not make a 10.95× cut survivable.

Distillation

Distillation trains the sliced network for one epoch against the frozen teacher, using the DINO, iBOT and DINOv2 losses (Caron et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Oquab et al., 2024) as distillation losses plus Minitron’s hidden-state matching: two terms on what the student says about a tile and two on what it computes on the way there (Figure 10). Each tile is seen as two global views, without DINO’s local crops (Caron et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021).

Written out, the objective is

\[ \mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\mathrm{DINO}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}+\alpha\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\right), \tag{4}\]

with every \(\lambda\) set to 1, so the only weight that matters is \(\alpha\), set automatically (Eq. 11). \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\) are cross-entropies, in nats, between distributions over 131,072 learned prototypes. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) are cosine distances between hidden states, each in \([0,2]\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\), summed over five taps, spans \([0,10]\). \(\alpha\) exists because the two families are on different scales.

Where the distributions come from

Each model’s features go through a projection head: a three-layer MLP down to a few hundred dimensions, L2 normalization and a bias-free layer of 131,072 prototypes. A softmax over the prototype scores turns a feature vector into a soft assignment over a learned vocabulary of visual patterns (Figure 11).

The prototypes start random, so no single one has to mean anything: the target is the pattern of agreement across all of them, a far richer signal than a label and a far looser constraint than matching hidden states.

The teacher’s distribution is sharpened by a low temperature and held away from collapse by a running center, while the student’s uses a flatter temperature. DINO relies on that asymmetry to keep the objective from degenerating (Caron et al., 2021), and with the teacher’s trunk frozen, it protects the heads and prototypes rather than the features.

Sharpening, centering, and the collapse failure mode

The teacher’s logits have a running center subtracted and are divided by a temperature that warms from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first fifth of the epoch. The student’s use a flat 0.1.

Sharpening alone would let every tile collapse onto one prototype, driving the loss to zero with features that carry no information. The center, a running average of the teacher’s logits, prevents this by pushing down any prototype that keeps winning.

One departure matters more than the rest. Prov-GigaPath ships no DINO or iBOT projection heads, and frozen random heads would make the targets arbitrary, so we apply exponential-moving-average copies of the student’s own heads to the teacher’s features, which makes the targets move during training.

The heads, the center and the neck Eq. 5

With \(c\) the running center, \(\tau_t\) the teacher temperature and \(\tau_s\) the student’s, one prototype comparison between a teacher logit vector \(a\) and a student logit vector \(b\) is

\[ H(a,b)=-\sum_{k=1}^{131{,}072}\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a-c}{\tau_{t}}\right)_{k}\log\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{b}{\tau_{s}}\right)_{k}. \tag{5}\]

Each head is a three-layer MLP, L2 normalization and a bias-free, weight-normalized prototype layer (Salimans and Kingma, 2016), with its own prototypes and center. The teacher-side heads are EMAs of the student’s, and the prototype layer is frozen for the first 7% of the epoch, the standard DINO guard against an unstable early codebook (Table 11).

A learned \(640\rightarrow1536\) neck between the student trunk and its heads lets both models run identically shaped heads. It is initialized as a scatter matrix, so at step zero each student channel lands in the teacher channel it was sliced from. The hidden-state projection below is a separate layer with the same shape and initialization.

The four terms

The prototype losses see only the two ends of the network, while the hidden-state losses reach inside it (Figure 12).

The hidden-state comparison, for a student state \(h_{s}\), a teacher state \(h_{t}\) and the shared projection, a matrix \(W\) and bias \(\beta\) that carry 640 channels up to 1,536, is

\[ d(h_{s},h_{t})=1-\cos\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{s})+\beta,\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{t})\big), \tag{6}\]

averaged over tokens. LayerNorming both sides first makes it about direction rather than magnitude. With this and \(H(a,b)\) from Eq. 5, the four terms are:

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\): the CLS token, across views
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[H(t^{1},s^{2})+H(t^{2},s^{1})\right] \tag{7}\]

The teacher’s CLS distribution on view 1 is the target for the student’s CLS on view 2, and vice versa. The crossing is the point: the student never matches a distribution computed from the crop it was shown, so it scores well only by describing the tissue in terms that survive a different crop and stain.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\): the 196 patch tokens, within a view
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}=\frac{1}{2P}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{p=1}^{196}H\!\left(t^{j}_{p},s^{j}_{p}\right) \tag{8}\]

The same comparison from a second head, at every patch position. Nothing crosses, because patch \(p\) of one crop does not show the same tissue as patch \(p\) of the other. Like DINOv2’s distillation recipe and H0-mini, we supervise all 196 patches rather than a masked subset (Oquab et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025). DINO supervises what the tile contains, iBOT where things are.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\): five hidden states
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{5} d\!\left(h^{(\ell),j}_{s},h^{(\ell),j}_{t}\right) \tag{9}\]

Five pairs: student blocks 6, 13, 19 and 26 against teacher blocks 13, 20, 26 and 33, plus the final tokens, summed over the five and averaged over the two views. Student block 13 is asked to reproduce teacher block 20 because it is teacher block 20, minus the channels, heads and MLP hidden dimensions cut from it. This term makes the run a repair job rather than a fresh training run.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\): the tensor entering block 0
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2} d\!\left(e^{j}_{s},e^{j}_{t}\right) \tag{10}\]

The same distance on the patch projection plus CLS token plus positional embedding, averaged over the two views. It is the only term that targets the layers before the first block directly, so the student cannot drift into a different input convention and compensate downstream.

Matching hidden states across unequal widths

The single learned map \(W,\beta\) in Eq. 6 projects every student state, at all five taps and the embedding, from 640 channels up to the teacher’s 1,536. It starts as a scatter matrix with zero bias, so student channel \(i\) writes into the teacher channel it was sliced from and the projection begins as an identity on the kept subspace, not a random map that must be learned before the hidden loss means anything.

Balancing the two objectives Eq. 11

Prototype cross-entropy and cosine distance are on different scales, and their ratio drifts, so any fixed \(\alpha\) is wrong almost everywhere. On the output side we balance \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\), the cross-entropy of Eq. 5 minus the teacher distribution’s entropy, summed over both heads and clamped at zero. Unlike the cross-entropy, this teacher-to-student KL falls to zero when the distributions agree. After each optimizer step we update EMAs of it and of the hidden loss and set

\[ \alpha\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{\mathrm{EMA}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\right]}{\mathrm{EMA}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}\right]},\;0,\;1000\right), \tag{11}\]

with \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) and EMA momentum 0.9. Because the ratio is lagged and detached from the graph, a noisy microbatch cannot re-weight its own loss. Minitron’s current-step ratio was too noisy at our batch size. Because the numerator is a divergence, \(\alpha\) stays near zero while the prototype distributions agree, however high the cross-entropy in Figure 13 sits.

The training run

The budget is one epoch of AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019): 3,854 warmup steps, then a cosine schedule from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-6}\), at 1,024 tiles per step for 77,085 steps, 78.9 million tiles in all. Sharded across eight A100s with PyTorch’s FSDP2 (Zhao et al., 2023), the run took 99 hours without interruption, about 790 GPU-hours.

The loss is a diagnostic, not a scoreboard: targets move with the student, the teacher temperature warms and \(\alpha\) follows a running ratio, so losses at different points are not on one scale. Most of the climb from 8 to 13 is the targets’ own entropy rising as they soften, and by the end only 1.9 of the prototype terms’ 10.9 is divergence, though iBOT’s divergence does rise in the second half. The repair shows up in the checkpoints instead (Figure 14).

The first tenth of the epoch, 7.9 million tiles, does almost all of the repair: HEST goes from 0.236 to 0.393, above the teacher’s 0.387, PLISM from 0.235 to 0.555 and the EVA patch-task mean from 0.590 to 0.830. HEST then stays within 0.002 of the teacher until six tenths of the way through and drifts to 0.382, and PLISM holds near 0.55 until seven tenths and falls to 0.520 as the learning rate anneals. The EVA mean wanders between 0.828 and 0.843, mostly because BACH and BreakHis are small (Table 6).

Some intermediate checkpoints beat the last, but choosing one by the benchmarks would bias the comparisons, so we report the end of the epoch. HEST did inform one earlier choice: the optimizer settings were picked over eleven alternatives in two-epoch runs on 100 TCGA slides, ranked mainly by HEST. All of this is one seed, and without a same-shape twin trained from random weights, we cannot tell how much of the fast repair comes from the inherited weights.

Next steps

TinyPath keeps nearly all of its teacher’s accuracy at a tenth of the size, giving up the most on HEST’s colon and rectum tasks, BreakHis and MoNuSAC. We cannot yet say how much of its quality comes from pruning rather than the distillation recipe. Next, we plan to run:

  1. Multiple seeds. One run shows the repair within a tenth of the epoch but cannot separate encoders a few thousandths apart.
  2. Slide-level cohorts TinyPath has not seen. It trained on nearly every slide in our TCGA cohorts, and Camelyon16, the one slide-level task outside TCGA, only asks whether a lymph node holds metastasis.
  3. An ablation of the hidden-state losses. They are the likeliest route by which the inherited weights speed up training, but the pruning map also reaches the prototype losses through the neck, so a clean test crosses hidden-state losses on or off with a neck initialized from the pruning map or at random.
  4. A two-step cut against this one-shot baseline. Minitron found that pruning to an intermediate model first beat a single cut for its most aggressive reduction (Muralidharan et al., 2024), and ours is more aggressive still.
Appendix A: Benchmark protocols

Which weights we score. TinyPath is scored from its live weights, not their EMA, reading its 640-dimensional features before the neck. DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) report the EMA copy. TinyPath’s EMA copy is not uniformly better: 0.3830 against 0.3822 on HEST, 0.534 against 0.520 on PLISM’s current mode and 0.840 against 0.837 on EVA’s patch tasks, but 0.564 against 0.569 on an earlier LUAD survival run. We keep one choice throughout.

HEST-Benchmark. Nine spatial-transcriptomics cohorts from nine organs. Each spot’s tile features are reduced to 256 dimensions with PCA, and a ridge regression predicts the 50 most variable genes. The score is Pearson correlation averaged over genes, over each task’s patient-disjoint folds (27 in all), then over the nine tasks. Every score but TinyPath’s and GigaPath-Flash’s is the current leaderboard’s. Our pipeline reproduces Prov-GigaPath’s nine-task mean exactly and every task to within 0.0001. The original 2024 paper reported 0.3853 on an older release.

EVA. kaiko.ai’s framework, pinned at version 0.4.5 with unchanged configurations. On the five patch tasks, BACH (Aresta et al., 2019), BreakHis (Spanhol et al., 2016), CRC (Kather et al., 2019), Gleason (Arvaniti et al., 2018) and PatchCamelyon (Veeling et al., 2018), a linear head on frozen CLS features is trained under five seeds and scored by balanced accuracy. BreakHis’s 339-image validation split puts its sampling standard error near 0.022. Camelyon16-small (Ehteshami Bejnordi et al., 2017) pools up to 1,000 patches per slide with an attention-MIL head over 20 runs, and MoNuSAC (Verma et al., 2021) segments nuclei with a convolutional decoder on patch tokens, scored by Dice over five runs. As on EVA’s leaderboard, BACH, BreakHis, CRC, Gleason and MoNuSAC are scored on the split that also selects the head checkpoint, so they are model-selection scores. Prov-GigaPath’s and H-optimus-0’s scores are the leaderboard’s, recomputed in March 2025 when kaiko.ai changed EVA’s learning-rate schedules and early-stopping patience. H0-mini’s paper predates that change and lacks BreakHis and Gleason, so we ran H0-mini ourselves, as we did GigaPath-Flash, which has no published EVA score. The change moved H-optimus-0’s scores on the patch tasks in H0-mini’s paper by at most 0.003. Through our pipeline, Prov-GigaPath lands within 0.005 of the leaderboard on the patch tasks and MoNuSAC and 0.011 below it on Camelyon16-small, a third of its run-to-run spread.

PLISM. The same 8,139 tissue positions imaged under 91 stain and scanner combinations. For every pair of acquisitions, each position’s two features are compared by cosine similarity and by top-10 retrieval, with no head trained. As in the leaderboard code, the score averages four medians over pairs, each rounded to three decimals (Table 5). Prov-GigaPath’s leaderboard entry (Owkin, 2025), 0.333, predates the benchmark’s Prov-GigaPath code, and how it was made is unpublished. Feeding the 224-pixel tiles in unresized reproduces it and all four of its medians, and we call this the older mode. The Prov-GigaPath code the benchmark added in May 2025 resizes each tile to 256 and center-crops back to 224, as in Prov-GigaPath’s README. In this current mode, never used for the leaderboard, our Prov-GigaPath run scores 0.320. Features are extracted in float16 autocast, as the benchmark does for Prov-GigaPath, and our Prov-GigaPath features match its output bit for bit on 256 sampled tiles. Our PyTorch port of the scorer matches the original on every score, and running the checkpoint sweep in TF32 moves no score by more than 0.001. H0-mini’s and H-optimus-0’s scores are leaderboard entries made with unresized input, and through our pipeline H0-mini reproduces its entry in every column. GigaPath-Flash is not on the leaderboard, and its model card prescribes Prov-GigaPath’s resize, so we run it in both modes.

Prov-GigaPath’s tasks. The paper scored most slide-level tasks on Providence’s private slides, so we rebuilt them on TCGA from its description. That gives six of its nine subtyping tasks, with esophagogastric reduced to two classes because TCGA has no gastroesophageal-junction cohort, and its four mutation panels: five LUAD genes, the same five pan-cancer, a 17-gene pan-cancer panel (the 18th marker, PD-L1, is measured by expression) and high tumor mutational burden split at the TCGA median. A gated attention-MIL head (Ilse et al., 2018) on frozen tile features is trained and scored over 10 patient-disjoint folds on subsets of 1,503 TCGA slides. The paper’s own numbers, from its LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023) on random splits, are not comparable, so Prov-GigaPath’s tile features go through the same head and folds as TinyPath’s.

PORPOISE. The histology-only survival model of Chen et al. (2022), retrained on TCGA-LUAD with the authors’ released labels and five folds, with every tile of a patient’s slides in one bag, and scored by Harrell’s c-index (Harrell et al., 1982). Our rebuild departs from the paper in three ways: 9 of its 453 patients are missing, tiles come from our own tissue detection and tiling, and each fold trains five head seeds rather than one. So we also run the paper’s ResNet-50 extractor through our rebuild, and that run lands inside the published 95% interval (Table 7). A fourth, slight difference: these runs floor survival-loss probabilities at float32’s machine epsilon, about \(1.19\times10^{-7}\), rather than the released code’s \(10^{-7}\). Our code now matches, but these runs were not retrained.

H0-mini. Scored through its 768-dimensional class token, as TinyPath is. Its paper concatenates the class token with the mean patch feature on HEST, so the class-token leaderboard entries used here, 0.3958 on HEST and 0.541 on PLISM, are not its paper’s numbers. It was distilled for 105,000 steps at batch 2,048 on 43 million tiles from the same TCGA slides as TinyPath, about 215 million tiles seen in all.

GigaPath-Flash. Scored through its 384-dimensional class token, as TinyPath is, from its released weights at a pinned revision and with its teacher’s preprocessing. On 36 tiles from EVA’s CRC task its features match Microsoft’s official GigaPath-Flash code bit for bit, in float32 and float16. It never saw TCGA.

Notation

The teacher \(T\) has depth 40 and width \(C_{t}=1536\), and the student \(S\) has depth 33 and width \(C_{s}=640\). Both turn a \(224\times224\) tile into \(P=196\) patch tokens plus one CLS token. \(a_{b,n,c}\) is a hooked activation at calibration example \(b\), token \(n\) and component \(c\), and \(\mathrm{LN}\) is a non-affine layer normalization applied token-wise.

The teacher and the student

The teacher is the published Prov-GigaPath tile encoder at a pinned revision, frozen throughout. The student inherits its patch size, token count and head width. Every other dimension comes from the architecture search, with the head count tied to width / 64 (Pruning).

The recorded mlp_ratio of 2.4 is the packed first projection, \(2\times768=1536\) wide because SwiGLU emits gate and value branches, over the width of 640. The MLP hidden size, 768, is counted after the branches combine, which is also where pruning ranks its dimensions.

TinyPath’s recipe borrows from Minitron, DINOv2 and H0-mini without reproducing any of them.
