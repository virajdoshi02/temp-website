Computational pathology runs on tiles. A whole-slide image is often 100,000 pixels on a side, far too large to process whole, so it is cut into tens of thousands of small tiles, and a frozen foundation model turns each tile into a feature vector. Everything downstream works from those vectors, including our own spatial-transcriptomics model. Our encoder has been the tile encoder of Prov-GigaPath (Xu et al., 2024), a billion-parameter vision transformer pretrained on more than a billion tiles of clinical slides. It describes H&E tissue remarkably well, but it is a large model to run once on every tile of every slide in every cohort.

There are two usual ways to get a smaller encoder. One is to pretrain a compact model from scratch, which throws away the billion-tile pretraining run that made the large one good. The other is to distill the large model into a standard small architecture, as H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) and GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026) do. Minitron (Muralidharan et al., 2024) showed a third way for language models: rank a trained model’s channels, attention heads, MLP neurons and layers by how much each contributes, cut the network down to the parts that matter, and then distill from the original to repair the damage. The student starts from the teacher’s own structure instead of rediscovering it, and needs a fraction of the training a model built from scratch would.

In this post we apply that recipe to Prov-GigaPath. One pass of activation-based structured pruning cuts it down to NanoPath, a dense ViT about 11× smaller that takes the same tiles and returns the same token grid, and a single epoch of distillation teaches what is left to cover for what was removed. We first show how NanoPath scores on public benchmarks, against its teacher and against H0-mini, and then walk through the pruning and the distillation. Benchmark protocols, full result tables and the training configuration are collected in the appendix.

Summary

  1. A 1.13-billion-parameter tile encoder becomes a 103.6-million-parameter one, and stays a dense ViT. Nothing is masked and nothing is zeroed. We rank residual channels, attention heads, SwiGLU neurons and whole blocks by their activations on real H&E tissue, search 861 shapes between 85M and 105M parameters for the best one, and slice it straight out of the teacher’s weights, a 10.95× compression.
  2. The cut on its own is not a working encoder. Before a single gradient step, the sliced network predicts gene expression on HEST at a mean Pearson correlation of 0.236, where the teacher reaches 0.387. Asked to find the same tissue scanned on another machine, it puts the match among its ten nearest neighbors 0.2% of the time, against the teacher’s 57%.
  3. Distillation repairs it with four losses at once. Two are prototype cross-entropies, on the CLS token and on all 196 patch tokens; two are cosine distances, on five hidden states and on the tensor entering the first block. One epoch covers 78.9 million tiles from 6,093 TCGA slides in 77,085 optimizer steps: 99 wall-clock hours on eight A100 GPUs, or about 790 GPU-hours.
  4. On published benchmarks NanoPath keeps nearly all of its teacher. It scores 0.382 against 0.387 on HEST, 98.6% of the teacher. On eva’s classification tasks it matches the teacher, averaging 0.8525 over the five leaderboard tasks we could run against 0.8524 published and 0.8505 in our run, though it trails our teacher run by 0.041 on BreakHis and by 0.037 on MoNuSAC nucleus segmentation. On PLISM it scores about 0.2 above its teacher, retrieving the same tissue across scanners and stains far more reliably: 0.530 against 0.333 with tiles fed in unresized, which reproduces Prov-GigaPath’s leaderboard entry exactly, and 0.520 against 0.320 with the resize the benchmark’s code now applies. H0-mini, an 86M-parameter student of a stronger teacher, trained on the same slides for about 2.7 times as many tiles seen, scores higher on HEST at 0.396 but keeps a smaller share of its teacher, 95%.
  5. Almost all of the repair happens in the first tenth of the epoch. By then NanoPath scores 0.393 on HEST, above its teacher, and from there it drifts down to 0.382 while our own tissue-of-origin probe keeps climbing. The training loss cannot show this, because its targets move with the student. How much of the fast repair the inherited weights deserve is still open, because there is no twin of the same shape trained from random weights under the same recipe (When the repair happens).

Results

To measure what NanoPath gives up, we score it almost entirely on benchmarks that other people designed and published, so the tasks, splits and scoring were fixed before NanoPath existed. Three of them score tile features directly: HEST predicts spatial gene expression from tissue (Jaume et al., 2024), eva classifies histology patches and slides and segments nuclei (kaiko.ai et al., 2024), and PLISM measures how far a feature moves when the same tissue is rescanned or restained (Ochi et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025). The rest work on whole slides: the PORPOISE survival model (Chen et al., 2022) and the subtyping and mutation tasks from the Prov-GigaPath paper (Xu et al., 2024), both rebuilt on public TCGA slides (Weinstein et al., 2013); the Prov-GigaPath paper scored most of its tasks on Providence’s own slides, which are not public. Alongside the paper’s tasks we score two simple ones of our own, tissue of origin and tile-level probes, and label them wherever they appear.

Every score comes from frozen features with at most a light head on top; no encoder is fine-tuned. NanoPath is scored from its live weights at the end of the epoch, with features read before the neck, the learned projection that feeds its distillation heads. Next to the teacher we report H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025), an 86M-parameter ViT-B distilled from Bioptimus’s H-optimus-0 (Saillard et al., 2024), run through the same tile-level pipelines. Rows marked published come from a benchmark’s public leaderboard or its source paper; rows marked ours, and every NanoPath row, are our own runs. Our Prov-GigaPath rows come from one rerun of every evaluation in October, so the teacher is scored on every task NanoPath is. Appendix A describes how each benchmark is scored, how closely our runs reproduce the published ones, and why we score live weights rather than their EMA average.

Tile-level benchmarks

On HEST and eva’s patch tasks NanoPath keeps nearly all of its teacher: 0.3822 against 0.3875 on HEST, which is 98.6%, and 0.837 on the five patch tasks against 0.843 for our teacher run and 0.841 published. The HEST gap is not spread evenly. NanoPath is ahead of the teacher on four of the nine tasks, and colon and rectum account for more than the whole deficit: it trails there by 0.030 and 0.029, while the other seven tasks together come out 0.012 ahead (Table 2). On eva it is ahead of our teacher run on Gleason, CRC and PatchCamelyon, by 0.010, 0.005 and 0.003, and behind on BACH by 0.004 and on BreakHis by 0.041 (Table 3). BreakHis is the noisiest task in the suite: its score moved by up to 0.07 between consecutive checkpoints and touched the published teacher’s 0.827 four tenths of the way through the epoch.

Beyond patch classification the picture splits. On Camelyon16-small, which pools a slide’s patches with attention-MIL, NanoPath scores 0.837 against 0.804 for our teacher run and 0.815 published, a lead of about one standard deviation of the teacher’s 20 runs; on the full Camelyon16, with ten times as many patches per slide, it scores 0.934 against 0.890. On MoNuSAC nucleus segmentation, which reads the patch-token map rather than the class token, it scores 0.644 against our teacher run’s 0.681 and the published 0.680, a gap of 0.037 that only BreakHis exceeds (Table 4). Averaged the way eva’s leaderboard averages, over the five of its classification tasks we could run, NanoPath comes to 0.8525, against 0.8524 for the published teacher and 0.8505 for our run.

PLISM is where NanoPath stops resembling its teacher, and it is the one benchmark with two settings for Prov-GigaPath. Its leaderboard entry, 0.333, predates the benchmark’s Prov-GigaPath code; our teacher run reproduces it exactly with tiles fed in at their native 224 pixels, which we call the older mode. The code the benchmark later added for Prov-GigaPath first resizes tiles to 256 and crops back to 224, as Prov-GigaPath’s README does; we call that the current mode, and in it our teacher run scores 0.320 (Appendix A). NanoPath is about 0.2 ahead in either mode, at 0.530 and 0.520 (Table 5). Asked to find the same tissue on a different scanner, the teacher puts the match among its ten nearest neighbors 57% of the time in the current mode and NanoPath 91%; across staining protocols, 11% against 27%. H0-mini shows the same jump over its own teacher, 0.541 against H-optimus-0’s published 0.480, which is the robustness gain its paper is built around (Filiot et al., 2025).

One plausible explanation is that distilling on a fixed corpus smooths away acquisition-specific detail the teacher still encodes. That is a useful property in a tile encoder, but it means NanoPath’s representation has changed, not merely shrunk, and robustness is not accuracy: the same model trails its teacher on HEST and on MoNuSAC.

When the repair happens

To see when the repair happens, we scored a checkpoint at every tenth of the epoch. At step zero the sliced network scores 0.236 on HEST and, by coincidence, 0.235 on PLISM, though the two mean different things: the HEST value is what a ridge probe recovers from the sliced network’s features, while the PLISM value is nearly all cosine similarity, with almost no retrieval behind it. The first tenth of the epoch, 7,708 steps and 7.9 million tiles, does almost all of the repair. HEST reaches 0.393, the best of the run and above the teacher’s 0.387, PLISM reaches 0.555, and the eva patch-task mean climbs from 0.590 to 0.830.

After that the benchmarks part ways. HEST stays within 0.002 of the teacher until six tenths of the way through and then drifts down to 0.382. PLISM holds near 0.55 until seven tenths and falls to 0.520 as the learning rate anneals. Our own tissue-of-origin probe, a linear probe on TCGA tiles simple enough to run at every checkpoint, improves at almost every one and finishes level with the teacher, 0.631 against 0.632 balanced accuracy, though on slides NanoPath trained on, so it tracks fit rather than generalization. The eva mean wanders between 0.828 and 0.843, mostly because BACH and BreakHis are small, and only the checkpoint at four tenths reaches the teacher’s 0.8425 (Table 6). Some intermediate checkpoints beat the last one, HEST at a tenth of the epoch and PLISM and eva at four tenths, but choosing a checkpoint by those scores would bias the very comparisons it was chosen on, and with nothing held out of distillation there is no independent set to choose by. We report the end of the epoch. HEST did inform an earlier decision, though: before this run, its optimizer settings were compared with eleven alternatives in two-epoch runs on 100 TCGA slides, ranked by HEST with ties broken by our tissue-of-origin probe and CKA, and kept.

What the curve cannot tell us is how much of this speed came from the pruned weights. Distilling from the teacher would carry over its pretraining whatever weights the student started from, so the real question is whether starting from the teacher’s own weights beats starting from random ones under the same targets. Answering it takes a twin of the same architecture trained from random weights under an identical recipe, which we have not yet run, so for now the fast repair describes the recipe rather than the inheritance. All of this is also one seed, so the numbers are descriptive point estimates.

Slide-level tasks

Slide-level benchmarks are where training overlap is hardest to avoid, because most public slide cohorts come from TCGA, and so does NanoPath’s training set. It trained on slides from 443 of the 444 patients in PORPOISE’s LUAD cohort and on 1,438 of the 1,503 labeled slides behind our Prov-GigaPath tasks, though never on their labels; Prov-GigaPath, pretrained on Providence slides, saw none of them.

On survival, the paper’s own ResNet-50 features (He et al., 2016), run through our rebuild of PORPOISE on TCGA-LUAD, reach a c-index of 0.574, inside the published interval around 0.548; NanoPath reaches 0.582 and Prov-GigaPath 0.553, both also inside it. The three fold means are at most 0.029 apart, a little over one standard error, and telling them apart would need a paired comparison on the same patients, which we have not run (Table 7). Histology alone predicts LUAD survival weakly: the paper’s own result was not significant, and all three encoders’ pooled 95% intervals are about 0.10 wide. Since NanoPath has seen nearly every slide in this cohort, what the result shows is similar point estimates with wide uncertainty, not that NanoPath matches a ResNet-50 or its teacher here, and not that it generalizes.

The Prov-GigaPath tasks are descriptive for the same reason. On the full labeled corpus NanoPath is level with its teacher on the paper’s five subtyping tasks, 0.909 against 0.910, and within 0.003 on our tissue-of-origin task; it is ahead on our tile probes, 0.914 against 0.905, and behind on mutation prediction by 0.007 macro AUROC and on breast ductal-versus-lobular subtyping by 0.033, a gap the neck mostly closes (Table 8). None of that measures generalization; it says the representation still carries what the tasks need.

Two other compact encoders

To put NanoPath in context, the natural comparisons are H0-mini and GigaPath-Flash, which both distill a billion-parameter pathology encoder into a standard small ViT. NanoPath is instead cut out of its teacher, so its shape and its starting weights both come from the model it imitates.

H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) is the strongest compact encoder we have measured on HEST: at 0.3957 it is above GigaPath itself. Its teacher is also stronger there, though. H-optimus-0 scores 0.4150 on the same leaderboard, so H0-mini keeps 95.3% of its teacher where NanoPath keeps 98.6% of its own; under H0-mini’s own protocol, the class token concatenated with the mean patch feature, the figure is 95.7%, 0.404 against 0.422. The two students train on the same 6,093 TCGA slides, though not on the same tiles, and differ in more than their teachers. H0-mini is a ViT-B with 14-pixel patches and four register tokens, distills through its teacher’s pretrained heads where NanoPath uses EMA student heads, and does not start from its teacher’s weights. It also saw about 215 million tiles, roughly five passes over 43 million, where NanoPath saw 78.9 million once, about 2.7 times fewer. The retention figures describe the two runs; they cannot say which of these differences matters. On eva the two split the tasks: H0-mini leads on BACH, CRC and Gleason, and NanoPath on BreakHis and, narrowly, PatchCamelyon (Table 3). H0-mini is also ahead on PLISM, 0.541 against NanoPath’s 0.530 in the older mode, where both see PLISM’s tiles unresized, and 0.520 in the current mode.

GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026) is the closer relative: the same GigaPath ViT-g teacher, distilled with the DINOv2 recipe, minus its KoLeo regularizer, into a 22M-parameter ViT-S/16 on Providence slides, then given its own 21M-parameter LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023). Its authors report it only through that slide encoder, on their own splits of PANDA prostate grading (Bulten et al., 2022) and EBRAINS brain tumor subtyping (Roetzer-Pejrimovsky et al., 2022), neither of which we have run, so none of its numbers can be set against ours. What does carry over is the ratio: GigaPath-Flash keeps 96.8% of its teacher’s average score on those two tasks at a fiftieth of the compute per slide. Its tile encoder is released as open weights under Apache 2.0, so nothing but time stands between us and running it through HEST, eva and PLISM.

How NanoPath is built

NanoPath comes out of two algorithms run back to back, and it helps to keep them apart because they do different jobs and fail in different ways. Pruning is one shot: it reads activations off the frozen teacher, decides what the student is, and copies every weight it keeps straight out of the teacher. Distillation is an ordinary training run that never touches the architecture, and it decides how well the student works (Figure 2). Minitron was written for language models, and a pathology ViT has no vocabulary logits to match and no perplexity to measure, so a few load-bearing pieces of the recipe had to be swapped out; Appendix D lists every substitution.

The teacher is a ViT-g/16, the giant vision transformer configuration of DINOv2 with 16-pixel patches (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024): 1,134,953,984 parameters in 40 transformer blocks on a residual stream 1,536 channels wide, with 24 attention heads of 64 dimensions each and 4,096 neurons in every SwiGLU branch (Shazeer, 2020). A 224×224 crop becomes 196 patch tokens plus a CLS token. Prov-GigaPath pretrained it on 1,384,860,229 tiles from 171,189 slides, H&E and immunohistochemistry together, a count its abstract rounds to 1.3 billion (Xu et al., 2024). We prune the tile encoder alone; Prov-GigaPath’s LongNet slide encoder is a separate model, and we leave it untouched.

The two stages draw on different data. Pruning works from 5,802 TCGA slides, split by patient before anything begins: 580 supply the tiles that score importance, another 580 score architecture candidates, and the probe that trains each candidate draws on 3,480 others, so the search never scores a candidate on tissue it was calibrated or trained on. Distillation uses a different, larger list, the 6,093 TCGA slides H0-mini was distilled on, and trains on every one of them. Nothing is held out, so NanoPath is judged on downstream benchmarks alone, and the results flag each benchmark whose slides it has seen.

Pruning: carving a student out of the teacher

Pruning takes the frozen teacher and hands back a smaller set of weights in three stages: score which structural units carry the signal, search for how many of each the budget affords, and slice the survivors out of the teacher’s tensors. Scoring and slicing take no gradients at all, and the search trains each candidate only long enough to rank it before discarding those weights. Attention, depth and feed-forward capacity do different jobs, and how a fixed budget should be divided between them is an empirical question, so each of the four axes is ranked on its own and the search decides how much of each survives (Figure 3). The axes are linked only through the shared budget and the head count, which we pin to the width divided by 64 so that attention width stays equal to residual width, as it is in the ViT we slice; Minitron’s language models let the two differ.

Scoring what the teacher actually uses

Weight magnitude is a poor proxy for importance: a large weight that rarely fires matters less than a small one that fires on everything. So we score activations, what each component emits when the teacher looks at real H&E tissue. One forward pass over the calibration tiles, with four families of hooks attached, produces every number the rest of the stage needs (Figure 4).

The three width axes share one reduction: average the absolute activation over tokens, then accumulate across calibration examples. Depth needs a different signal. Minitron offers two: how much perplexity rises when a block is deleted, and block importance, a measure it takes from ShortGPT that asks how much work a block does (Men et al., 2024; Muralidharan et al., 2024). A ViT has no perplexity, so we use the second. A transformer block is a residual update, so we measure how far its output has rotated away from its input; a block whose output is nearly parallel to its input is barely rewriting the representation and scores near zero. The full pass over all four axes, 4,096 tiles from the 580 calibration slides at batch size 32, takes under two wall-clock minutes on one GPU, most of it spent loading the teacher.

The importance scores in detail Eq. 1–3

For an activation \(a_{b,n,c}\) at calibration example \(b\), token \(n\) and component \(c\), the three width axes first collapse the token axis,

\[ r_{b,c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|a_{b,n,c}\right|, \tag{1}\]

and then take an L2 norm across examples:

\[ s_{c}=\sqrt{\sum_{b} r_{b,c}^{2}}. \tag{2}\]

Both choices earn their place. Taking absolute values before averaging stops a component that fires hard in opposite directions on different tokens from cancelling itself to zero; a signed mean would score it as dead. Accumulating squares and taking the root only at the end makes the score independent of how the calibration tiles were divided into batches, which matters when the same ranking has to be reproducible across machines.

Heads get one extra step before that reduction: the packed attention output is reshaped into 24 head vectors and each is reduced to its own L2 norm, so a head is scored as a unit rather than as 64 loose coordinates. Neurons are hooked on the input to the second MLP projection, the one place where the gate and value branches have already been combined. That matters because SwiGLU’s first projection produces both branches in one packed tensor: neuron \(i\) owns rows \(i\) and \(i+m\) of that tensor, where \(m\) is the branch width, so both rows have to survive together or the neuron is meaningless. Channels are hooked at both LayerNorms of every block and summed over the whole trunk, because the residual stream is global and a channel cannot be kept in one block and dropped in another.

Depth is scored by block importance, which ShortGPT calls block influence (Men et al., 2024), the mean cosine change across the residual path,

\[ \mathrm{BI}_{i}=1-\mathbb{E}_{b,n}\left[\cos\left(x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{in}},x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)\right], \tag{3}\]

averaged over every token of every calibration tile. Blocks are then ranked on \(\mathrm{BI}\) across the whole network rather than within any local window.

Choosing a shape, not a sparsity ratio

The budget is 100 million backbone parameters, allowing 5% over and 15% under, and 861 shapes fit inside it. They are not interchangeable: a wide, shallow student and a narrow, deep one can have the same parameter count and very different representations. So the search builds every hardware-friendly shape in the budget by top-\(k\) against the rankings above, and then has to work out which candidate kept the most of the teacher, a question a parameter count cannot answer.

Centered Kernel Alignment compares the pattern of pairwise similarities among examples rather than individual coordinates. Two representations of different widths score highly if they organize the same images the same way.

That is the expensive part. Each candidate gets three scores of increasing cost, the last a thousand-step training probe followed by CKA against the teacher on held-out tiles (Kornblith et al., 2019), and the ranking uses that last score. The winner is 640 channels, 33 blocks, 10 heads and 768 neurons, at 103,630,848 parameters. Probing all 861 candidates takes about five and a half wall-clock hours on eight GPUs, or 44 GPU-hours, and the probe earns its cost. Before it, the winner ranks 325th on CKA, while the three shapes one-shot CKA likes best, all wide and shallow at 1,408 or 1,472 channels over six or seven blocks, reach only 0.49 to 0.53 after the probe, against the winner’s 0.77.

The search space and the three ranking signals

The grid is every hardware-friendly shape: widths from 64 to 1,536 in steps of 64, depths from 1 to 40, and SwiGLU widths from 256 to 4,096 in steps of 256, with head width pinned at 64. That is 15,360 shapes, of which 861 land between 85 and 105 million parameters. Turning a shape into a candidate is pure top-\(k\): blocks come from the global block-importance ranking, residual channels from the single global channel ranking, and heads and neurons from their own per-block rankings. No candidate is trained into existence; each is a straight slice of the teacher.

The three signals, cheapest first:

  • Subspace cosine. The candidate’s pooled features against the teacher’s, restricted to the surviving channels. It needs no training and is nearly free. Every candidate went on to the probe anyway, so here it is a diagnostic rather than a filter.
  • Linear CKA. Compares the geometry of the two feature sets without caring about width or channel order, so a 640-wide candidate can be scored against the 1,536-wide teacher directly.
  • CKA after a 1,000-step probe. Each candidate trains briefly on the hidden-state losses alone, cycling through 2,048 tiles from the 3,480 probe slides, and CKA is recomputed on 512 tiles from the 580 architecture-validation slides, which appear in neither calibration nor the probe’s training tiles. This is the ranking key, with parameter error as the tiebreak.

The margin at the top is thin. The winner scores 0.7721, and the next two, 768 channels over 25 blocks and 704 over 25, score 0.7717 and 0.7715, so the choice among them is closer to a tie than a verdict. The weakest candidate in the budget, 1,536 channels over three blocks, scores 0.347.

The probe deliberately leaves out the DINO and iBOT heads. A 131,072-way prototype head is still mostly noise after 1,000 steps, and including it would rank candidates on how their heads happened to initialize rather than on the backbone we are trying to choose. The probe checkpoints are then discarded, and production re-slices the original frozen teacher from scratch.

Making the cut

Slicing is where structured pruning either works or quietly corrupts the model, and it is almost entirely bookkeeping: every index has to be applied to every tensor it touches, or the network ends up subtly wrong in a way no shape check will catch. Block importance kept blocks 0–5 and 13–39, so the seven blocks it dropped form one contiguous band in the early-middle of the trunk (Figure 5). We do not force the first and last blocks to survive; they survive because the metric ranked them highly.

What each index touches
  • The 640 surviving residual channels are applied to the patch projection, the CLS token, the positional embedding, every LayerNorm, every block’s input and output, and the final norm, which is everywhere the residual stream appears.
  • A surviving head takes its rows out of Q, K and V and the matching columns of the attention output projection.
  • A surviving neuron takes its paired gate and value rows out of the packed first projection and the matching column of the second.
  • Surviving blocks keep their own LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) and LayerScale (Touvron et al., 2021) parameters, in the teacher’s original order.

This is a one-shot prune, which is what Minitron recommends (Muralidharan et al., 2024): in its experiments, removing one layer at a time with retraining in between did worse than a single cut. The exception was its most aggressive reduction, where pruning 15B to 8B and then 8B to 4B beat going from 15B to 4B in one step. Our reduction is more aggressive still, so a two-step cut may help here, but a single cut keeps the experiment legible: everything NanoPath holds at step zero came from the teacher, and nothing came from search-time optimization.

What the cut costs

Before a single training step, the sliced network is a bad model. On HEST it predicts gene expression at a mean Pearson correlation of 0.236 against the teacher’s 0.387. On PLISM the failure is starker: shown the same tissue scanned on two different machines, it puts the matching tile among its ten nearest neighbors 0.2% of the time, where the teacher manages 57% (Figure 6).

PLISM also shows what is wrong. Two scans of the same tissue position give the sliced network features with a median cosine similarity of 0.936, far above the teacher’s 0.553, and yet it cannot tell one position from another: its top-10 retrieval across scanners is 0.002, so a tile’s twin is almost never among its ten nearest neighbors. For almost every tile, then, at least ten unrelated positions sit closer than its twin does. The likeliest reading is that the network maps most tiles to nearly the same direction, though we did not measure unrelated pairs directly. Activation-based importance does not make a 10.95× structural cut survivable on its own, and whether a better ranking could is not something this experiment tests. What the cut gives distillation is a place to start, and a tenth of an epoch is enough to take it past the teacher on HEST (Figure 1).

Distillation: teaching the student back

Distillation begins where pruning stopped, with a set of weights that were tuned to sit next to weights that no longer exist. Nothing structural changes from here: NanoPath has its shape, and the single epoch that follows goes into making the survivors work without the parts that were removed. The losses come from DINO, iBOT and DINOv2 (Caron et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Oquab et al., 2024), used here as distillation losses against a frozen external teacher rather than as self-supervised objectives, alongside the hidden-state matching that Minitron adds. Four terms supervise the student, two on what it says about a tile and two on what it computes on the way there (Figure 7). Every tile is seen as two global views and nothing else; the local crops of DINO’s multi-crop scheme (Caron et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021) are not used.

Written out, the objective is

\[ \mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\mathrm{DINO}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}+\alpha\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\right), \tag{4}\]

with every \(\lambda\) set to 1, so the only weight that matters is \(\alpha\), and we do not set that by hand either. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\) are cross-entropies between distributions over 131,072 learned prototypes, so they constrain what the student says about a tile. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) are cosine distances between hidden states, so they constrain what it computes. The first pair is measured in nats. Each hidden-state distance lies in \([0,2]\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\), a sum over five taps, spans \([0,10]\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) spans \([0,2]\). That is why the two families cannot simply be added, and why \(\alpha\) exists.

Where the distributions come from

Saying that two of the terms compare probability distributions raises an obvious question, because nothing inside a ViT produces one. A transformer emits vectors. The distributions are manufactured, and seeing how is most of what it takes to read the first two terms.

Each model’s features go through a projection head: a three-layer MLP that squeezes the feature down to a few hundred dimensions and normalizes it onto the unit sphere, followed by a single bias-free layer holding 131,072 vectors of its own, each a unit direction times a learned length that starts at one. Those vectors are the prototypes. Because the feature is unit length, each output of that layer is a cosine similarity scaled by that prototype’s length, and a softmax over all of them turns one feature vector into a distribution: a soft assignment of the tile across a learned vocabulary of 131,072 visual patterns (Figure 8).

A prototype loss says sort this tile the way I would; a hidden-state loss says compute it the way I would. The first tolerates a student that reaches the teacher’s answer by another route; the second does not. Using both is the compromise.

Nobody writes down what the vocabulary means. The prototypes are ordinary trainable parameters that start random, so “prototype 41,006” ends up standing for whatever the run made it stand for. That is fine, because no single prototype has to mean anything: the target is the pattern of agreement across all of them at once. Two tiles of the same tissue land on overlapping sets of prototypes, and the student’s job is to reproduce that spread. It is a far richer signal than a label, and a far looser constraint than matching the direction of the teacher’s hidden states through a learned projection, which is what the other two terms ask for.

The two sides of the cross-entropy are deliberately treated differently. The teacher’s distribution is sharpened by a very low temperature, which is why its bars in Figure 8 are nearly one spike, and held away from collapse by a running center. The student’s is softmaxed at a flatter temperature and is always chasing the more confident of the two. DINO relies on that asymmetry to keep the objective from degenerating (Caron et al., 2021), and with the teacher’s trunk frozen, it is the heads and prototypes, not the features, that it protects here.

Sharpening, centering, and the collapse failure mode

The teacher’s logits have a running center subtracted and are divided by a temperature that warms from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first 16,519 steps, about a fifth of the epoch; the student’s are softmaxed at a flat 0.1. The low teacher temperature sharpens: it turns a set of similar scores into something close to a vote for a single prototype.

Sharpening on its own has a degenerate solution, and it is the failure mode everyone who trains DINO meets. The cheapest way for two networks to agree on a sharp distribution is for every tile in the dataset to land on the same prototype: the loss goes to zero and the features carry no information. Centering prevents it. The center is a running average of the teacher’s logits, so any prototype that keeps winning gets its score pushed down for the next batch, which drives the distribution back toward uniform. Sharpening pulls toward one spike, centering pushes toward flat, and DINO works in the tension between them.

There is an awkward detail underneath all of this. GigaPath ships a backbone, not DINO and iBOT projection heads, and the prototype losses need heads to produce a distribution. Freezing a randomly initialized head would make the targets arbitrary, so instead we keep exponential-moving-average copies of the student’s own heads and apply them to the frozen teacher’s features. The catch is that the targets move during training. That is a real departure from the published recipes, and the main reason we do not read much into the value of the training loss.

The heads, the center and the neck Eq. 5

With \(c\) the running center, \(\tau_t\) the teacher temperature and \(\tau_s\) the student’s, one prototype comparison between a teacher logit vector \(a\) and a student logit vector \(b\) is

\[ H(a,b)=-\sum_{k=1}^{131{,}072}\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a-c}{\tau_{t}}\right)_{k}\log\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{b}{\tau_{s}}\right)_{k}. \tag{5}\]

Both heads are three-layer MLPs with hidden width 2,048, a bottleneck of 384 for DINO and 256 for iBOT, L2 normalization, and a bias-free layer with weight normalization (Salimans and Kingma, 2016) onto 131,072 prototypes. Each head carries its own prototypes and its own center. The center is an exponential moving average of the teacher’s logits with momentum 0.9, and the teacher-side heads are exponential moving averages of the student’s heads, with a momentum that rises from 0.994 to 1.0 along a cosine over the epoch. The final prototype layer is frozen for the first 5,504 steps, 7% of the epoch, the standard DINO guard against an unstable codebook early in training. After that each prototype’s length trains along with its direction, as in DINOv2.

One shape detail makes any of this possible. The student’s trunk is 640 channels wide and the teacher’s is 1,536, so a learned \(640\rightarrow1536\) neck sits between the student trunk and its heads. Both models then run identically shaped heads over identically shaped features, and the neck is initialized as a scatter matrix, so at step zero each student channel lands in the teacher channel it was sliced from. The neck is a separate layer from the hidden-state projection described below, even though both are \(640\rightarrow1536\) and both start from the same channel map.

The four terms

Everything above is machinery shared by the first two terms. What distinguishes all four is which pairs of tensors get compared, and where in the two networks they are read. The prototype losses only ever see the two ends of the network; the hidden-state losses reach inside it (Figure 9).

The hidden-state comparison, for a student state \(h_{s}\), a teacher state \(h_{t}\) and the shared projection, a matrix \(W\) and bias \(\beta\) that carry 640 channels up to 1,536, is

\[ d(h_{s},h_{t})=1-\cos\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{s})+\beta,\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{t})\big), \tag{6}\]

averaged over tokens. LayerNorming both sides first makes it a question about direction rather than magnitude. With that and \(H(a,b)\) from Eq. 5 in hand, the four terms are:

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\): the CLS token, across views
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[H(t^{1},s^{2})+H(t^{2},s^{1})\right] \tag{7}\]

Every tile is cropped and jittered into two views. The teacher’s CLS distribution on view 1 is the target for the student’s CLS on view 2, and the other way round. The crossing is the entire point: the student never gets to match a distribution computed from the crop it was actually shown, so the only way to score well is to describe the tissue in terms that survive a different crop and a different stain.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\): the 196 patch tokens, within a view
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}=\frac{1}{2P}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{p=1}^{196}H\!\left(t^{j}_{p},s^{j}_{p}\right) \tag{8}\]

The same comparison from a second head with its own prototypes and its own center, run at every patch position. Nothing crosses here, because patch \(p\) of one crop is not looking at the same tissue as patch \(p\) of the other, so teacher view \(j\) is matched against student view \(j\). Following DINOv2’s distillation recipe and H0-mini, we supervise all 196 patches rather than a masked subset (Oquab et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025), which makes this the dense term: 196 targets per view against DINO’s one. DINO supervises what the tile contains as a whole; iBOT forces the student to preserve where things are.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\): five hidden states
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{5} d\!\left(h^{(\ell),j}_{s},h^{(\ell),j}_{t}\right) \tag{9}\]

Five pairs: student blocks 6, 13, 19 and 26 against teacher blocks 13, 20, 26 and 33, plus the student’s final tokens against the teacher’s, summed over the five and averaged over the two views. The pairing is where the objective reuses the pruning map. Student block 13 is asked to reproduce teacher block 20 because it is teacher block 20, minus the channels, heads and neurons that were cut out of it. This is the term that makes the run a repair job rather than a fresh training run.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\): the tensor entering block 0
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2} d\!\left(e^{j}_{s},e^{j}_{t}\right) \tag{10}\]

The same distance on the patch projection plus CLS token plus positional embedding, before any attention has happened, averaged over the two views. It gets its own term rather than a sixth entry above because it is the only term that targets the part of the student in front of the first block directly; the others reach it only through backpropagation. Without it, the student could drift into a different input convention and then satisfy every other term by compensating for it downstream.

Matching hidden states across unequal widths

The two hidden-state terms have a problem the two prototype terms do not. Prototype logits are 131,072-dimensional on both sides, because both heads were built to that width, so the distributions are directly comparable. Hidden states are 640 channels on the student and 1,536 on the teacher, and there is no cosine to take between vectors of different lengths. LayerNorming both sides fixes the scale but not the dimension.

So the single learned map in Eq. 6, \(W\) with its bias \(\beta\), projects every student state up to teacher width. It is initialized as a scatter matrix with a zero bias: student channel \(i\) writes into the teacher channel it was sliced from, and every deleted coordinate starts at zero. The projection therefore begins as an identity on the kept subspace instead of a random map that has to be learned before the hidden loss means anything. The same map is shared across all five taps and the embedding term.

Balancing the two objectives Eq. 11

Prototype cross-entropy and cosine distance are not on the same scale, and their ratio drifts over training, so any fixed value of \(\alpha\) is wrong almost everywhere. The output-side quantity we balance against is not the cross-entropy itself but \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\): the cross-entropy of Eq. 5 less the entropy of the teacher’s own distribution, summed over the DINO and iBOT heads and clamped at zero. That is the KL divergence from teacher to student, and unlike the cross-entropy it falls to zero when the two distributions agree. After each optimizer step we update exponential moving averages of that divergence and of the hidden loss and set

\[ \alpha\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{\mathrm{EMA}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\right]}{\mathrm{EMA}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}\right]},\;0,\;1000\right), \tag{11}\]

with \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) and EMA momentum 0.9. Because the ratio is lagged and detached from the graph, a noisy microbatch cannot re-weight its own loss; the first window runs on the output terms alone, and from then on the two families stay comparable. Minitron uses the current-step ratio rather than a lagged one, which we found too noisy at this batch size. And because the numerator is a divergence, \(\alpha\) is near zero whenever the two prototype distributions nearly agree, however high the cross-entropy plateau in Figure 10 sits, and grows only as they come apart: the hidden-state terms take their weight from output disagreement, not from the absolute level of the prototype loss.

The training run

The budget is a single epoch of AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019): a warmup over 3,854 steps, then a cosine schedule from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-6}\), with an effective batch of 1,024 tiles per optimizer step, for 77,085 steps, 78.9 million tiles in all. Sharded across eight A100 GPUs with FSDP2, PyTorch’s fully sharded data parallelism (Zhao et al., 2023), the run took 99 wall-clock hours without interruption, about 790 GPU-hours, at about 220 tiles a second across all eight GPUs.

That chart is a training diagnostic, not a scoreboard. With targets that move as the student moves, a teacher temperature that keeps warming for the first fifth of the epoch, and a hidden-state weight set by a running ratio, the loss at one point in the run is not on the same scale as the loss at another. Most of the climb from 8 back to 13 comes from the targets rather than the student. A prototype cross-entropy is the entropy of the target distribution plus the divergence the student can reduce, and the targets soften as the teacher temperature warms and spread over more prototypes as the EMA heads develop. By the end of the epoch the two prototype terms sum to about 10.9, of which only 1.9 is divergence. iBOT’s divergence does rise in the second half, though, and \(\alpha\) rises with it, so the split is not clean. The repair itself shows up in the checkpoints rather than in this curve (Figure 1).

Next steps

NanoPath has 9.1% of its teacher’s parameters and the same input geometry, keeps nearly all of its teacher’s HEST score, matches its published average on eva’s classification tasks, and is steadier across scanners and stains. What it gives up is concentrated in BreakHis and in MoNuSAC nucleus segmentation, the one benchmark here that reads patch tokens rather than the class token. What we cannot say yet is how much of the result came from pruning rather than from the distillation recipe. Next, we plan to run:

  1. A random-initialized twin at this scale. The same architecture trained from random weights under the same recipe is the only way to measure what the inherited weights contributed, and it is the first thing to run.
  2. GigaPath-Flash on the same tile benchmarks. It is a student of the same teacher distilled into a standard ViT-S rather than cut out of it, its weights are public, and nothing published yet puts the two approaches side by side.
  3. Multiple seeds. Every number here comes from one training run, which is enough to see the repair happen within a tenth of the epoch and not enough to separate encoders whose scores differ by a few thousandths.
  4. Slide-level cohorts NanoPath has not seen. NanoPath trained on nearly every slide in the PORPOISE and Prov-GigaPath cohorts, and the one slide-level task outside TCGA reported here, Camelyon16, asks only whether a lymph node holds metastasis. A larger non-TCGA cohort with clinical labels would let slide-level results do more than describe.
  5. An ablation of the hidden-state losses. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) are the only terms that reach inside the network, so they are the most likely route by which the inherited weights would speed up training, and the easiest to test by removing. The pruning map also reaches the prototype losses through the neck, so a clean version crosses the two: hidden-state losses on or off, with the neck initialized from the pruning map or at random. Those four runs separate the two routes and show whether they interact.
  6. A held-out set for choosing checkpoints and settings. Several intermediate checkpoints beat the last one on HEST, PLISM and eva, but without data held out for the purpose we report the end of the epoch rather than pick one by the benchmarks it would then be judged on. The same set would take HEST’s place in ranking optimizer settings.
  7. A two-step cut against this one-shot baseline. Minitron found that pruning to an intermediate model first beat a single cut for its most aggressive reduction (Muralidharan et al., 2024), and ours is more aggressive still.
Appendix A: Benchmark protocols

Which weights we score. NanoPath is scored from its live weights rather than their EMA average, with its 640-dimensional features read before the neck, the learned 640→1,536 projection between its trunk and its distillation heads (Distillation). DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) report the EMA copy instead, and ours is not uniformly better or worse: it scores 0.3830 against the live 0.3822 on HEST, 0.534 against 0.520 on PLISM in the current mode and 0.840 against 0.837 on eva’s patch tasks, but 0.564 against 0.569 on an earlier LUAD survival run that caps each slide at 4,096 tiles, the only survival run scored both ways. We hold to one choice throughout rather than take the better of the two per benchmark.

HEST-Benchmark. Nine spatial-transcriptomics cohorts from nine organs. Each spot’s tile is encoded, the features are reduced to 256 dimensions with PCA, and a ridge regression predicts the 50 most variable genes. The score is the Pearson correlation for each gene, averaged over genes, over each task’s patient-disjoint folds (27 in all), and finally over the nine tasks with equal weight. Our GigaPath run reproduces the current leaderboard: the nine-task mean agrees exactly and every task is within 0.0001 of its entry. The original 2024 paper reported 0.3853 on an older release of the benchmark.

eva. kaiko.ai’s evaluation framework, pinned at version 0.4.5 with its configurations unchanged. On the five patch tasks, BACH (Aresta et al., 2019), BreakHis (Spanhol et al., 2016), CRC (Kather et al., 2019), Gleason (Arvaniti et al., 2018) and PatchCamelyon (Veeling et al., 2018), a linear head is trained on frozen CLS features under five seeds, and the score is balanced accuracy. BreakHis is breast tumor images at 40× in four classes, two benign and two malignant, scored on its 339-image validation split, which puts the sampling standard error near 0.022 however tight the seed spread looks. Camelyon16-small asks whether a lymph-node slide from Camelyon16 (Ehteshami Bejnordi et al., 2017) holds metastasis, from up to 1,000 tissue patches per slide pooled by an attention-MIL head over 20 runs; MoNuSAC (Verma et al., 2021) segments four classes of nuclei with a convolutional decoder on the last block’s patch tokens, scored by Dice over five runs. As on eva’s leaderboard, a task without a test split is scored on the validation split that also picks which checkpoint of its head is kept, and MoNuSAC’s validation split is the release’s test split, so the BACH, BreakHis, CRC, Gleason and MoNuSAC scores are model-selection scores rather than held-out test results. Our teacher run covers every task. It lands within 0.005 of the leaderboard on the five patch tasks and on MoNuSAC, and 0.011 below it on Camelyon16-small, about a third of its run-to-run spread (Table 3, Table 4); an earlier run gave 0.819 on BreakHis, against 0.829 here. eva’s leaderboard average leaves out BACH and needs three tasks we do not have, so there is no official average to quote, and every average here says which tasks it covers.

PLISM. The same 8,139 tissue positions imaged under 91 combinations of staining protocol and slide scanner. For every pair of acquisitions, the two feature vectors of each position are compared by cosine similarity and by whether one retrieves the other among its ten nearest neighbors. The score is the mean of four medians over pairs, each rounded to three decimals first, as the benchmark’s leaderboard code does: cosine similarity over all 4,095 pairs, and top-10 retrieval across scanners, across stains, and across both. No head is trained. Prov-GigaPath has two settings here. Its leaderboard entry (Owkin, 2025), 0.333, went up in March 2025, before the benchmark had any Prov-GigaPath code, and how it was made is not published; 224-pixel tiles fed in unresized reproduce it, and we call this the older mode. The Prov-GigaPath code the benchmark added that May resizes each tile to 256 and center-crops it back to 224, the preprocessing in Prov-GigaPath’s own README; this is the current mode, and the leaderboard entry was never regenerated with it. We run Prov-GigaPath and NanoPath in both modes (Table 5). Features are extracted in float16 autocast, which is how the benchmark’s own extraction runs Prov-GigaPath, and on a sample of 256 tiles our teacher features are bit-identical to its output. In the older mode our teacher run matches all four published medians to three decimals and the published score itself; in the current mode it scores 0.320. Our scorer is a PyTorch port of the benchmark’s, and rescoring these runs with the benchmark’s own scorer leaves every score unchanged. The checkpoint sweep and H0-mini ran in TF32, which changes no score by more than 0.001. H0-mini keeps the unresized input the benchmark’s code gives it, which reproduces its leaderboard entry in every column.

Prov-GigaPath’s tasks. The paper scored most of its slide-level tasks on Providence’s own slides, which are not public, so we rebuilt them from its description on public TCGA slides. That gives six of its nine subtyping tasks: the other three need cohorts TCGA does not have, and esophagogastric subtyping has two classes because TCGA has no gastroesophageal-junction cohort. It also gives the paper’s four mutation panels: five LUAD genes, the same five genes pan-cancer, a 17-gene pan-cancer panel (the paper’s 18th marker, PD-L1, is measured by expression rather than mutation), and high tumor mutational burden split at the TCGA median. Frozen tile features feed a gated attention-MIL head (Ilse et al., 2018), trained and scored over 10 patient-disjoint folds on 1,503 labeled TCGA slides, 1,438 of which NanoPath trained on. The paper ran its LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023) with random splits, so its numbers are context rather than a matched comparison. Two tasks in Table 8 are our own, and both are deliberately simple. Tissue of origin asks which of 17 TCGA projects a slide came from; its label comes free with every slide, so it needs no annotation and covers all 1,503. The tile probes fit a linear classifier on 256 tiles per slide. With no slide-level model to train, they score the encoder alone and are cheap enough to rerun at every checkpoint. We run them on tissue of origin and the five subtyping tasks; Table 8 averages each classifier’s predictions over the slide, and Table 6 tracks the tissue-of-origin probe tile by tile. Both are quick checks that the features still separate tissue and cancer types, not published benchmarks.

PORPOISE. The histology-only survival model of Chen et al. (2022), retrained on TCGA-LUAD with the authors’ released labels and five folds and scored by Harrell’s c-index (Harrell et al., 1982). We rebuilt its cohort as closely as the public slides allow: 444 of the paper’s 453 patients, every tile of a patient’s slides in one bag, and five head seeds per fold. The paper’s own ResNet-50 feature extractor, run through that pipeline, lands inside the published 95% interval (Table 7), which is what makes the encoder rows readable. (One slight difference remains: in these runs the survival loss floors predicted probabilities at float32’s machine epsilon, about \(1.19\times10^{-7}\), rather than at the released code’s \(10^{-7}\), which changes the loss only for probabilities below that floor. Our code now matches; these runs were not retrained.)

H0-mini. Encoded through its 768-dimensional class token, as NanoPath is. Its paper scores HEST with the class token concatenated to the mean patch feature, so the class-token leaderboard entries we reproduce, 0.3957 on HEST against the published 0.3958 and 0.541 on PLISM, are not the numbers in its paper. It was distilled for 105,000 steps at batch 2,048 on 43 million tiles from the same TCGA slides as NanoPath, about 215 million tiles seen in all.

Appendix D: Where we depart from the papers Table 17

This implementation borrows from Minitron, DINOv2 and H0-mini without reproducing any of them. The substitutions are forced by the model we are compressing and the checkpoint we were given, and each one narrows what the experiment can claim.

The consequences run in one direction: DINO and iBOT losses are not language-model KL, CKA is not biological performance, and moving EMA targets mean the objective is not a fixed yardstick. Each of these is fine as a tool for building the student. None of them is evidence about the student, which is why the evaluation is a separate stack, built mostly on published benchmarks, though not on entirely separate data: the results flag each benchmark whose slides NanoPath trained on.
