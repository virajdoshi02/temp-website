TinyPath is a 103.6-million-parameter tile encoder for H&E pathology. We cut it out of the tile encoder of Prov-GigaPath (Xu et al., 2024), a 1.13-billion-parameter vision transformer pretrained on more than a billion tiles of clinical slides, and distilled it from that model for one epoch. TinyPath is a standard dense ViT that takes the same tiles and returns the same token grid, and on published benchmarks it keeps nearly all of its teacher: 98.6% of its HEST score, its EVA classification average, and far higher robustness to changes of scanner and stain.

A tile encoder runs on every tile of every slide, often tens of thousands per slide, so its size matters. The usual way to shrink one is to distill it into a standard small architecture, as H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) and GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026) do. TinyPath follows Minitron (Muralidharan et al., 2024) instead: rank the teacher’s channels, attention heads, MLP hidden dimensions and blocks by how much each contributes, slice the best shape that fits the budget straight out of its weights, and distill from the original to repair the damage.

Summary

  1. TinyPath is Prov-GigaPath cut to a tenth of its size. It keeps 640 of 1,536 residual channels and 33 of 40 blocks, and within each block 10 of 24 attention heads and 768 of the MLP’s 4,096 hidden dimensions: 103.6 million parameters against 1.13 billion, a 10.95× compression. Nothing is masked or zeroed; every weight is sliced straight out of the teacher’s.
  2. It keeps nearly all of its teacher on published benchmarks. It scores 0.382 against 0.387 on HEST, 98.6% of the teacher, and on EVA’s classification tasks averages 0.8525 over the five leaderboard tasks we could run, against the teacher’s published 0.8524. On EVA it trails the teacher only on BreakHis, by 0.039, and on MoNuSAC nucleus segmentation, by 0.036.
  3. It is far more robust to scanner and stain changes. On PLISM it scores 0.530 against 0.333 with tiles fed in unresized, which reproduces Prov-GigaPath’s leaderboard entry, and 0.520 against 0.320 with the resize the benchmark’s code now applies, which the leaderboard predates, so that teacher score is our own run. With that resize it finds the same tissue on another scanner among its ten nearest neighbors 91% of the time, against the teacher’s 57%.
  4. H0-mini scores higher on HEST but keeps less of its teacher. The 86M-parameter student of the stronger H-optimus-0, trained on the same slides for about 2.7 times as many tiles seen, scores 0.396 on HEST, 95% of its teacher.
  5. It took about 835 GPU-hours to build. Pruning, including a search over 861 candidate shapes, takes 44 GPU-hours. Distillation is one epoch over 78.9 million tiles from 6,093 TCGA slides, 99 hours on eight A100 GPUs. The sliced network alone scores 0.236 on HEST, and the first tenth of the epoch takes it past the teacher (The training run).

Results

We score TinyPath on published benchmarks, whose tasks, splits and scoring were fixed before it existed. HEST predicts spatial gene expression from tissue (Jaume et al., 2024), EVA classifies patches and slides and segments nuclei (kaiko.ai et al., 2024), and PLISM measures how far a feature moves when the same tissue is rescanned or restained (Ochi et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025). On whole slides we run the PORPOISE survival model (Chen et al., 2022) and the Prov-GigaPath paper’s subtyping and mutation tasks, which we rebuilt on public TCGA slides (Weinstein et al., 2013) because most of the paper’s slides are private. Every score comes from frozen features with at most a light head on top, and next to the teacher we report H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025), an 86M-parameter ViT-B distilled from Bioptimus’s H-optimus-0 (Saillard et al., 2024). Every other model’s score is the published one wherever the protocol behind it matches ours. Where it does not, we say so and add our own run: for Prov-GigaPath under the resize PLISM’s code now applies, for H0-mini on EVA, whose paper predates EVA’s current settings, and on the slide-level tasks, which we had to rebuild. Appendix A gives each protocol and Appendix B every score.

Tile-level benchmarks

On HEST and EVA TinyPath is close to its teacher: it keeps 98.6% of its HEST score, and averaged the way EVA’s leaderboard averages, over the five of its classification tasks we could run, it comes to 0.8525, against the teacher’s published 0.8524. On PLISM it scores about 0.2 higher (Figure 1).

What TinyPath gives up is concentrated in a few tasks (Figure 2). On HEST, colon and rectum account for more than the whole deficit. On EVA it trails only on BreakHis, the noisiest task in the suite, whose score moved by up to 0.07 between consecutive checkpoints, and on MoNuSAC nucleus segmentation, the one benchmark here that reads patch tokens rather than the class token. Its largest lead is on Camelyon16-small, which pools a slide’s patches with attention-MIL, though only by about one standard deviation of its 20 runs.

The PLISM gain holds with the resize the benchmark’s code now applies, and most of it is retrieval: TinyPath is far more likely to find the same tissue under another scanner or stain (Figure 3). H0-mini shows the same jump over its own teacher, the robustness gain its paper is built around (Filiot et al., 2025). One plausible explanation is that distilling on a fixed corpus smooths away acquisition-specific detail the teacher still encodes. Either way, TinyPath’s representation has changed rather than merely shrunk, and robustness is not accuracy: the same model trails its teacher on HEST and MoNuSAC.

Slide-level tasks

Most public slide cohorts come from TCGA, and so does TinyPath’s training set: it trained on slides from 443 of the 444 patients in PORPOISE’s LUAD cohort and on 1,438 of the 1,503 slides behind our Prov-GigaPath tasks, though never on their labels. Prov-GigaPath, pretrained on Providence slides, saw none of them, so these results describe the representation rather than measure generalization.

No published Prov-GigaPath score matches the protocols we ran here: PORPOISE never evaluated it, and its own paper ran its LongNet slide encoder with random splits, mostly on Providence’s private slides. Our PORPOISE rebuild also departs from the PORPOISE paper in its cohort, tiling and head seeds (Appendix A). So here, as on PLISM’s current mode, the teacher’s scores are our own runs. On the Prov-GigaPath tasks TinyPath’s subtyping and mutation averages come within 0.01 AUROC of its teacher’s, with single tasks moving by up to 0.02 in either direction, and it trails by 0.033 on breast ductal-versus-lobular subtyping (Figure 2, Table 8). On LUAD survival, TinyPath, Prov-GigaPath and the PORPOISE paper’s own ResNet-50 features (He et al., 2016), all three run through our rebuild, land inside the interval the paper published for ResNet-50, within a little over one standard error of each other (Table 7). Telling them apart would need a paired comparison on the same patients, which we have not run, and histology alone predicts LUAD survival weakly: the paper’s own result was not significant.

Two other compact encoders

H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) is the strongest compact encoder here on HEST, above Prov-GigaPath itself, but its teacher is stronger too: it keeps 95.4% of H-optimus-0’s score (95.7% under its own protocol, which concatenates the class token with the mean patch feature), where TinyPath keeps 98.6% of Prov-GigaPath’s. Task by task, TinyPath keeps the larger share on seven of HEST’s nine tasks. On EVA’s patch tasks the two keep nearly the same share of their teachers’ means, 99.5% and 99.8%, and on PLISM, where both beat their teachers, TinyPath reaches 159% of its teacher’s score and H0-mini 113% (Figure 4). The two students train on the same 6,093 TCGA slides but differ in more than their teachers: H0-mini is a ViT-B with 14-pixel patches and four register tokens, distills through its teacher’s pretrained projection heads, which Prov-GigaPath does not ship, does not start from its teacher’s weights, and saw about 215 million tiles, 2.7 times as many as TinyPath. The retention figures cannot say which difference matters.

GigaPath-Flash (Usuyama et al., 2026) is the closer relative: the same teacher, distilled with the DINOv2 recipe, minus its KoLeo regularizer, into a 22M-parameter ViT-S/16 on Providence slides, then given its own 21M-parameter LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023). Its authors report it only through that slide encoder, on their own splits of PANDA prostate grading (Bulten et al., 2022) and EBRAINS brain tumor subtyping (Roetzer-Pejrimovsky et al., 2022), neither of which we have run, so its numbers cannot be set against ours; on those two tasks it keeps 96.8% of its teacher’s average score at a fiftieth of the compute per slide.

How TinyPath is built

TinyPath comes out of two stages run back to back. Pruning reads activations off the frozen teacher, decides the student’s shape and copies every weight it keeps straight out of the teacher. Distillation is an ordinary training run that leaves the architecture alone and makes the pruned network work again (Figure 5). Minitron was written for language models, and a ViT has no vocabulary logits to match and no perplexity to measure, so a few pieces of its recipe had to be swapped out; Appendix D lists them.

The teacher is a ViT-g/16, DINOv2’s giant configuration with 16-pixel patches (Dosovitskiy et al., 2021; Oquab et al., 2024): 40 transformer blocks on a residual stream 1,536 channels wide, each with 24 attention heads of 64 dimensions and a SwiGLU MLP (Shazeer, 2020) whose hidden size, the width between its two projections, is 4,096. Prov-GigaPath pretrained it on 1.38 billion tiles from 171,189 slides (Xu et al., 2024). Prov-GigaPath’s LongNet slide encoder is a separate model, and we leave it untouched.

Pruning works from a 5,802-slide TCGA split made by patient and uses 4,640 of them: 580 supply the tiles that score importance, another 580 score architecture candidates, and 3,480 train the short probe each candidate gets, so no candidate is scored on tissue it was calibrated or trained on; the other 1,162 were held out. Distillation trains on all 6,093 TCGA slides H0-mini was distilled on, with nothing held out.

Pruning

Pruning runs in three steps: score which structural units carry the signal, search for how many of each the budget affords, and slice the survivors out of the teacher’s tensors. How a fixed budget should be divided between attention, depth and feed-forward capacity is an empirical question, so each of the four axes is ranked on its own and the search decides how much of each survives (Figure 6). The head count is pinned to the width divided by 64, so attention width stays equal to residual width, as in the teacher.

Scoring what the teacher actually uses

We score activations rather than weights, since a large weight that rarely fires matters less than a small one that fires on everything. One forward pass over 4,096 calibration tiles, with hooks on every block, scores all four axes in under two minutes on one GPU (Figure 7). The width axes average each component’s absolute activation over tokens and accumulate it across tiles. For depth, Minitron offers two signals: the rise in perplexity when a block is deleted, which a ViT does not have, and block importance, which it takes from ShortGPT (Men et al., 2024; Muralidharan et al., 2024). Block importance measures how far a block rotates its input, so a block whose output is nearly parallel to its input scores near zero.

The importance scores in detail Eq. 1–3

For an activation \(a_{b,n,c}\) at calibration example \(b\), token \(n\) and component \(c\), the three width axes first collapse the token axis,

\[ r_{b,c}=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=1}^{N}\left|a_{b,n,c}\right|, \tag{1}\]

and then take an L2 norm across examples:

\[ s_{c}=\sqrt{\sum_{b} r_{b,c}^{2}}. \tag{2}\]

The absolute value stops a component that fires in opposite directions on different tokens from cancelling itself to zero, and accumulating squares before taking the root makes the score independent of how the tiles were batched, so the ranking reproduces across machines.

Heads get one extra step before that reduction: the packed attention output is reshaped into 24 head vectors and each is reduced to its own L2 norm, so a head is scored as a unit rather than as 64 loose coordinates. MLP hidden dimensions are hooked on the input to the MLP’s second projection, the one place where the gate and value branches have already been combined. That matters because SwiGLU’s first projection produces both branches in one packed tensor: hidden dimension \(i\) owns rows \(i\) and \(i+m\) of that tensor, where \(m\) is the MLP hidden size, so both rows have to survive together or the dimension is meaningless. Channels are hooked at both LayerNorms of every block and summed over the whole trunk, because the residual stream is global and a channel cannot be kept in one block and dropped in another.

Depth is scored by block importance, which ShortGPT calls block influence (Men et al., 2024), the mean cosine change across the residual path,

\[ \mathrm{BI}_{i}=1-\mathbb{E}_{b,n}\left[\cos\left(x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{in}},x^{(i)}_{\mathrm{out}}\right)\right], \tag{3}\]

averaged over every token of every calibration tile. Blocks are then ranked on \(\mathrm{BI}\) across the whole network rather than within any local window.

Choosing a shape, not a sparsity ratio

The budget is 100 million backbone parameters, from 5% over to 15% under, and 861 shapes fit it. A wide, shallow student and a narrow, deep one can have the same parameter count and very different representations, so the search builds every shape in the budget by top-\(k\) against the rankings, trains each for a 1,000-step probe, and ranks them by CKA against the teacher on held-out tiles (Kornblith et al., 2019), which compares the pattern of similarities among images and so works across widths. The winner is 640 channels, 33 blocks, 10 heads and an MLP hidden size of 768, at 103,630,848 parameters. Probing all 861 takes about five and a half hours on eight GPUs, and the probe earns its cost: untrained CKA would not have picked the winner, which scores lowest of the eventual top five before the probe and highest after it.

The search space and the three ranking signals

The grid is every hardware-friendly shape: widths from 64 to 1,536 in steps of 64, depths from 1 to 40, and MLP hidden sizes from 256 to 4,096 in steps of 256, with head width pinned at 64. That is 15,360 shapes, of which 861 land between 85 and 105 million parameters. Turning a shape into a candidate is pure top-\(k\): blocks come from the global block-importance ranking, residual channels from the single global channel ranking, and heads and MLP hidden dimensions from their own per-block rankings. No candidate is trained into existence; each is a straight slice of the teacher.

The three signals, cheapest first:

  • Subspace cosine. The candidate’s pooled features against the teacher’s, restricted to the surviving channels. It is nearly free, and since every candidate went on to the probe, here it is only a diagnostic.
  • Linear CKA. Compares the geometry of the two feature sets without caring about width or channel order, so a 640-wide candidate can be scored against the 1,536-wide teacher directly.
  • CKA after a 1,000-step probe. Each candidate trains briefly on the hidden-state losses alone, cycling through 2,048 tiles from the 3,480 probe slides, and CKA is recomputed on 512 tiles from the 580 architecture-validation slides, which appear in neither calibration nor the probe’s training tiles. This is the ranking key, with parameter error as the tiebreak.

The margin at the top is thin. The winner scores 0.7721, and the next two, 768 channels over 25 blocks and 704 over 25, score 0.7717 and 0.7715, so the choice among them is closer to a tie than a verdict. Before the probe the same five shapes score between 0.2988 and 0.3095 with the winner lowest, and after it the winner also has the lowest probe loss of the five, 1.181 against 1.217 to 1.494.

The probe leaves out the DINO and iBOT heads: a 131,072-way prototype head is still mostly noise after 1,000 steps and would rank candidates on how their heads happened to initialize. The probe checkpoints are then discarded, and production re-slices the original frozen teacher.

Making the cut

Slicing is bookkeeping: every index has to reach every tensor it touches, or the network ends up subtly wrong in a way no shape check will catch. Block importance kept blocks 0–5 and 13–39, so the seven it dropped form one contiguous band in the early-middle of the trunk (Figure 8); the first and last blocks were not forced to survive.

What each index touches
  • The 640 surviving residual channels are applied to the patch projection, the CLS token, the positional embedding, every LayerNorm, every block’s input and output, and the final norm, which is everywhere the residual stream appears.
  • A surviving head takes its rows out of Q, K and V and the matching columns of the attention output projection.
  • A surviving MLP hidden dimension takes its paired gate and value rows out of the packed first projection and the matching column of the second.
  • Surviving blocks keep their own LayerNorm (Ba et al., 2016) and LayerScale (Touvron et al., 2021) parameters, in the teacher’s original order.

The cut is made in one shot, as Minitron recommends for all but its most aggressive reduction (Muralidharan et al., 2024), so everything TinyPath holds at step zero came from the teacher rather than from search-time training.

What the cut costs

Before a single training step, the sliced network is a bad model. On HEST it predicts gene expression at a mean Pearson correlation of 0.236 against the teacher’s 0.387, and shown the same tissue on two different scanners it puts the match among its ten nearest neighbors 0.2% of the time, where the teacher manages 57% (Figure 9).

PLISM also shows what is wrong. Two scans of the same tissue position give the sliced network features with a median cosine similarity of 0.936, far above the teacher’s 0.553, yet for almost every tile at least ten unrelated positions sit closer than its twin. The likeliest reading is that the network maps most tiles to nearly the same direction, though we did not measure unrelated pairs directly. Activation-based importance does not make a 10.95× structural cut survivable on its own, and whether a better ranking could is not something this experiment tests; what the cut gives distillation is a place to start.

Distillation

Distillation leaves the architecture alone and trains the sliced network for one epoch against the frozen teacher. The losses come from DINO, iBOT and DINOv2 (Caron et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2022; Oquab et al., 2024), used as distillation losses rather than self-supervised objectives, plus the hidden-state matching that Minitron adds: four terms in all, two on what the student says about a tile and two on what it computes on the way there (Figure 10). Each tile is seen as two global views; DINO’s local crops (Caron et al., 2020; Caron et al., 2021) are not used.

Written out, the objective is

\[ \mathcal{L}=\lambda_{\mathrm{DINO}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}+\alpha\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\right), \tag{4}\]

with every \(\lambda\) set to 1, so the only weight that matters is \(\alpha\), which is set automatically (Eq. 11). \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\) are cross-entropies between distributions over 131,072 learned prototypes, measured in nats. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) are cosine distances between hidden states, each distance in \([0,2]\), so \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\), a sum over five taps, spans \([0,10]\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) spans \([0,2]\). The two families are on different scales, which is why \(\alpha\) exists.

Where the distributions come from

Each model’s features go through a projection head: a three-layer MLP down to a few hundred dimensions, L2 normalization, and a bias-free layer holding 131,072 vectors, each a unit direction times a learned length that starts at one. Those vectors are the prototypes. Each output of that layer is a cosine similarity scaled by a prototype’s length, and a softmax over all of them turns one feature vector into a soft assignment of the tile across a learned vocabulary of visual patterns (Figure 11).

The prototypes start random, so no single one has to mean anything: the target is the pattern of agreement across all of them at once. That is a far richer signal than a label, and a far looser constraint than matching the direction of the teacher’s hidden states, which is what the other two terms ask for.

The teacher’s distribution is sharpened by a low temperature, which is why its bars in Figure 11 are nearly one spike, and held away from collapse by a running center; the student’s is softmaxed at a flatter temperature. DINO relies on that asymmetry to keep the objective from degenerating (Caron et al., 2021), and with the teacher’s trunk frozen, it protects the heads and prototypes rather than the features.

Sharpening, centering, and the collapse failure mode

The teacher’s logits have a running center subtracted and are divided by a temperature that warms from 0.04 to 0.07 over the first 16,519 steps, about a fifth of the epoch; the student’s are softmaxed at a flat 0.1.

Sharpening alone has a degenerate solution: every tile lands on the same prototype, the loss goes to zero and the features carry no information. Centering prevents it. The center is a running average of the teacher’s logits, so a prototype that keeps winning gets its score pushed down for the next batch, which drives the distribution back toward uniform.

One departure matters more than the rest. Prov-GigaPath ships its backbone without DINO and iBOT projection heads, and frozen random heads would make the targets arbitrary, so we apply exponential-moving-average copies of the student’s own heads to the teacher’s features. The targets therefore move during training, which is the main reason we read little into the value of the training loss.

The heads, the center and the neck Eq. 5

With \(c\) the running center, \(\tau_t\) the teacher temperature and \(\tau_s\) the student’s, one prototype comparison between a teacher logit vector \(a\) and a student logit vector \(b\) is

\[ H(a,b)=-\sum_{k=1}^{131{,}072}\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{a-c}{\tau_{t}}\right)_{k}\log\operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{b}{\tau_{s}}\right)_{k}. \tag{5}\]

Both heads are three-layer MLPs with hidden width 2,048, a bottleneck of 384 for DINO and 256 for iBOT, L2 normalization, and a bias-free layer with weight normalization (Salimans and Kingma, 2016) onto 131,072 prototypes. Each head carries its own prototypes and its own center. The center is an exponential moving average of the teacher’s logits with momentum 0.9, and the teacher-side heads are exponential moving averages of the student’s heads, with a momentum that rises from 0.994 to 1.0 along a cosine over the epoch. The final prototype layer is frozen for the first 5,504 steps, 7% of the epoch, the standard DINO guard against an unstable codebook early in training. After that each prototype’s length trains along with its direction, as in DINOv2.

The student’s trunk is 640 channels wide and the teacher’s is 1,536, so a learned \(640\rightarrow1536\) neck sits between the student trunk and its heads, letting both models run identically shaped heads. It is initialized as a scatter matrix, so at step zero each student channel lands in the teacher channel it was sliced from. The neck is a separate layer from the hidden-state projection below, though both are \(640\rightarrow1536\) and start from the same channel map.

The four terms

The four terms differ in which tensors they compare and where: the prototype losses see only the two ends of the network, while the hidden-state losses reach inside it (Figure 12).

The hidden-state comparison, for a student state \(h_{s}\), a teacher state \(h_{t}\) and the shared projection, a matrix \(W\) and bias \(\beta\) that carry 640 channels up to 1,536, is

\[ d(h_{s},h_{t})=1-\cos\!\big(W\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{s})+\beta,\,\mathrm{LN}(h_{t})\big), \tag{6}\]

averaged over tokens. LayerNorming both sides first makes it a question about direction rather than magnitude. With that and \(H(a,b)\) from Eq. 5 in hand, the four terms are:

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}\): the CLS token, across views
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DINO}}=\tfrac{1}{2}\left[H(t^{1},s^{2})+H(t^{2},s^{1})\right] \tag{7}\]

The teacher’s CLS distribution on view 1 is the target for the student’s CLS on view 2, and the other way round. The crossing is the point: the student never matches a distribution computed from the crop it was shown, so it can only score well by describing the tissue in terms that survive a different crop and stain.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}\): the 196 patch tokens, within a view
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{iBOT}}=\frac{1}{2P}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{p=1}^{196}H\!\left(t^{j}_{p},s^{j}_{p}\right) \tag{8}\]

The same comparison from a second head with its own prototypes and center, run at every patch position. Nothing crosses here, because patch \(p\) of one crop is not looking at the same tissue as patch \(p\) of the other. Following DINOv2’s distillation recipe and H0-mini, we supervise all 196 patches rather than a masked subset (Oquab et al., 2024; Filiot et al., 2025), so where DINO supervises what the tile contains as a whole, iBOT makes the student preserve where things are.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\): five hidden states
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2}\sum_{\ell=1}^{5} d\!\left(h^{(\ell),j}_{s},h^{(\ell),j}_{t}\right) \tag{9}\]

Five pairs: student blocks 6, 13, 19 and 26 against teacher blocks 13, 20, 26 and 33, plus the student’s final tokens against the teacher’s, summed over the five and averaged over the two views. Student block 13 is asked to reproduce teacher block 20 because it is teacher block 20, minus the channels, heads and MLP hidden dimensions that were cut out of it. This is the term that makes the run a repair job rather than a fresh training run.

\(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\): the tensor entering block 0
\[ \mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_{j=1}^{2} d\!\left(e^{j}_{s},e^{j}_{t}\right) \tag{10}\]

The same distance on the patch projection plus CLS token plus positional embedding, before any attention has happened, averaged over the two views. It gets its own term because it is the only one that targets the part of the student in front of the first block directly; the others reach it only through backpropagation. Without it, the student could drift into a different input convention and then satisfy every other term by compensating for it downstream.

Matching hidden states across unequal widths

Prototype logits are 131,072-dimensional on both sides, but hidden states are 640 channels on the student and 1,536 on the teacher, and there is no cosine between vectors of different lengths. So the single learned map in Eq. 6, \(W\) with its bias \(\beta\), projects every student state up to teacher width. It is initialized as a scatter matrix with a zero bias: student channel \(i\) writes into the teacher channel it was sliced from, and every deleted coordinate starts at zero. The projection therefore begins as an identity on the kept subspace instead of a random map that has to be learned before the hidden loss means anything. The same map is shared across all five taps and the embedding term.

Balancing the two objectives Eq. 11

Prototype cross-entropy and cosine distance are not on the same scale, and their ratio drifts over training, so any fixed value of \(\alpha\) is wrong almost everywhere. The output-side quantity we balance against is not the cross-entropy itself but \(D_{\mathrm{KL}}\): the cross-entropy of Eq. 5 less the entropy of the teacher’s own distribution, summed over the DINO and iBOT heads and clamped at zero. That is the KL divergence from teacher to student, and unlike the cross-entropy it falls to zero when the two distributions agree. After each optimizer step we update exponential moving averages of that divergence and of the hidden loss and set

\[ \alpha\leftarrow\mathrm{clip}\left(\frac{\mathrm{EMA}\left[D_{\mathrm{KL}}\right]}{\mathrm{EMA}\left[\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}\right]},\;0,\;1000\right), \tag{11}\]

with \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{hidden}}=\lambda_{\mathrm{IS}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{emb}}\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) and EMA momentum 0.9. Because the ratio is lagged and detached from the graph, a noisy microbatch cannot re-weight its own loss; the first window runs on the output terms alone, and from then on the two families stay comparable. Minitron uses the current-step ratio rather than a lagged one, which we found too noisy at this batch size. And because the numerator is a divergence, \(\alpha\) is near zero whenever the two prototype distributions nearly agree, however high the cross-entropy plateau in Figure 13 sits, and grows only as they come apart.

The training run

The budget is one epoch of AdamW (Loshchilov and Hutter, 2019): 3,854 warmup steps, then a cosine schedule from \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-6}\), at 1,024 tiles per optimizer step for 77,085 steps, 78.9 million tiles in all. Sharded across eight A100 GPUs with FSDP2, PyTorch’s fully sharded data parallelism (Zhao et al., 2023), the run took 99 hours without interruption, about 790 GPU-hours.

That chart is a training diagnostic, not a scoreboard: the targets move with the student, the teacher temperature keeps warming for the first fifth of the epoch, and \(\alpha\) follows a running ratio, so the loss at one point in the run is not on the same scale as at another. Most of the climb from 8 back to 13 comes from the targets rather than the student. A prototype cross-entropy is the entropy of the target plus the divergence the student can reduce, and the targets soften as the temperature warms and spread over more prototypes as the EMA heads develop; by the end of the epoch the two prototype terms sum to about 10.9, of which only 1.9 is divergence. iBOT’s divergence does rise in the second half, though, and \(\alpha\) rises with it, so the split is not clean. The repair shows up in the checkpoints instead (Figure 14).

The first tenth of the epoch, 7.9 million tiles, does almost all of the repair: HEST goes from 0.236 to 0.393, the best of the run and above the teacher’s 0.387, PLISM from 0.235 to 0.555, and the EVA patch-task mean from 0.590 to 0.830. After that the benchmarks part ways. HEST stays within 0.002 of the teacher until six tenths of the way through and then drifts down to 0.382, and PLISM holds near 0.55 until seven tenths and falls to 0.520 as the learning rate anneals. The EVA mean wanders between 0.828 and 0.843, mostly because BACH and BreakHis are small (Table 6).

Some intermediate checkpoints beat the last one, but with nothing held out of distillation there is no independent set to choose one by, and choosing by the benchmarks would bias the comparisons, so we report the end of the epoch. HEST did inform an earlier decision, though: before this run, its optimizer settings were compared with eleven alternatives in two-epoch runs on 100 TCGA slides, ranked by HEST with ties broken by CKA and an internal tissue-of-origin probe, and kept. All of this is one seed, and without a twin of the same shape trained from random weights under the same recipe, the fast repair describes the recipe rather than the inherited weights.

Next steps

TinyPath keeps nearly all of its teacher at a tenth of the size, giving up the most on BreakHis and on MoNuSAC, the one benchmark here that reads patch tokens rather than the class token. What we cannot say yet is how much of that came from pruning rather than from the distillation recipe. Next, we plan to run:

  1. Multiple seeds. One training run is enough to see the repair happen within a tenth of the epoch, not to separate encoders whose scores differ by a few thousandths.
  2. Slide-level cohorts TinyPath has not seen. It trained on nearly every slide in our survival and TCGA slide-level cohorts, and the one slide-level task outside TCGA, Camelyon16, asks only whether a lymph node holds metastasis. A larger non-TCGA cohort with clinical labels would let slide-level results do more than describe.
  3. An ablation of the hidden-state losses. \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{IS}}\) and \(\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{emb}}\) are the only terms that reach inside the network, so they are the likeliest route by which the inherited weights would speed up training. The pruning map also reaches the prototype losses through the neck, so a clean test crosses the two: hidden-state losses on or off, with the neck initialized from the pruning map or at random.
  4. A two-step cut against this one-shot baseline. Minitron found that pruning to an intermediate model first beat a single cut for its most aggressive reduction (Muralidharan et al., 2024), and ours is more aggressive still.
Appendix A: Benchmark protocols

Which weights we score. TinyPath is scored from its live weights rather than their EMA average, with its 640-dimensional features read before the 640→1,536 neck (Distillation). DINOv2 (Oquab et al., 2024) and H0-mini (Filiot et al., 2025) report the EMA copy instead, and ours is not uniformly better or worse: it scores 0.3830 against the live 0.3822 on HEST, 0.534 against 0.520 on PLISM in the current mode and 0.840 against 0.837 on EVA’s patch tasks, but 0.564 against 0.569 on an earlier LUAD survival run that caps each slide at 4,096 tiles. We hold to one choice throughout rather than take the better of the two per benchmark.

HEST-Benchmark. Nine spatial-transcriptomics cohorts from nine organs. Each spot’s tile is encoded, the features are reduced to 256 dimensions with PCA, and a ridge regression predicts the 50 most variable genes. The score is the Pearson correlation for each gene, averaged over genes, over each task’s patient-disjoint folds (27 in all), and finally over the nine tasks with equal weight. Every score but TinyPath’s is the current leaderboard’s, which our pipeline reproduces: run through it, Prov-GigaPath matches the nine-task mean exactly and every task to within 0.0001. The original 2024 paper reported 0.3853 on an older release of the benchmark.

EVA. kaiko.ai’s evaluation framework, pinned at version 0.4.5 with its configurations unchanged. On the five patch tasks, BACH (Aresta et al., 2019), BreakHis (Spanhol et al., 2016), CRC (Kather et al., 2019), Gleason (Arvaniti et al., 2018) and PatchCamelyon (Veeling et al., 2018), a linear head is trained on frozen CLS features under five seeds, and the score is balanced accuracy. BreakHis is breast tumor images at 40× in four classes, two benign and two malignant, scored on its 339-image validation split, which puts the sampling standard error near 0.022 however tight the seed spread looks. Camelyon16-small asks whether a lymph-node slide from Camelyon16 (Ehteshami Bejnordi et al., 2017) holds metastasis, from up to 1,000 tissue patches per slide pooled by an attention-MIL head over 20 runs; MoNuSAC (Verma et al., 2021) segments four classes of nuclei with a convolutional decoder on the last block’s patch tokens, scored by Dice over five runs. As on EVA’s leaderboard, a task without a test split is scored on the validation split that also picks which checkpoint of its head is kept, and MoNuSAC’s validation split is the release’s test split, so the BACH, BreakHis, CRC, Gleason and MoNuSAC scores are model-selection scores rather than held-out test results. Prov-GigaPath’s and H-optimus-0’s scores are the leaderboard’s (Table 3, Table 4), which kaiko.ai recomputed in March 2025 when it changed EVA’s learning-rate schedules and early-stopping patience. H0-mini’s paper ran EVA before that change and has no BreakHis or Gleason score, so next to its published scores we report our own run of H0-mini at the pinned version (Table 3). The change itself is small: it moved none of H-optimus-0’s scores on the three patch tasks in that paper by more than 0.003. Run through our pipeline, Prov-GigaPath lands within 0.005 of the leaderboard on the five patch tasks and on MoNuSAC, and 0.011 below it on Camelyon16-small, about a third of its run-to-run spread. EVA’s leaderboard average leaves out BACH and needs three tasks we do not have, so there is no official average to quote, and every average here says which tasks it covers.

PLISM. The same 8,139 tissue positions imaged under 91 combinations of staining protocol and slide scanner. For every pair of acquisitions, the two feature vectors of each position are compared by cosine similarity and by whether one retrieves the other among its ten nearest neighbors. The score is the mean of four medians over pairs, each rounded to three decimals first, as the benchmark’s leaderboard code does: cosine similarity over all 4,095 pairs, and top-10 retrieval across scanners, across stains, and across both. No head is trained. Prov-GigaPath has two settings here. Its leaderboard entry (Owkin, 2025), 0.333, went up in March 2025, before the benchmark had any Prov-GigaPath code, and how it was made is not published; 224-pixel tiles fed in unresized reproduce it, and we call this the older mode. The Prov-GigaPath code the benchmark added that May resizes each tile to 256 and center-crops it back to 224, the preprocessing in Prov-GigaPath’s own README; this is the current mode, and the leaderboard entry was never regenerated with it. We run TinyPath in both modes, and report our own Prov-GigaPath run only in the current one, which has no published score (Table 5). Features are extracted in float16 autocast, which is how the benchmark’s own extraction runs Prov-GigaPath, and on a sample of 256 tiles our teacher features are bit-identical to its output. In the older mode our teacher run matches all four published medians to three decimals and the published score itself; in the current mode it scores 0.320. Our scorer is a PyTorch port of the benchmark’s, and rescoring these runs with the benchmark’s own scorer leaves every score unchanged. The checkpoint sweep ran in TF32, which changes no score by more than 0.001. H0-mini’s and H-optimus-0’s scores are their leaderboard entries, made with the unresized input the benchmark’s code gives them; run that way through our pipeline, H0-mini reproduces its entry in every column.

Prov-GigaPath’s tasks. The paper scored most of its slide-level tasks on Providence’s own slides, which are not public, so we rebuilt them from its description on public TCGA slides. That gives six of its nine subtyping tasks: the other three need cohorts TCGA does not have, and esophagogastric subtyping has two classes because TCGA has no gastroesophageal-junction cohort. It also gives the paper’s four mutation panels: five LUAD genes, the same five genes pan-cancer, a 17-gene pan-cancer panel (the paper’s 18th marker, PD-L1, is measured by expression rather than mutation), and high tumor mutational burden split at the TCGA median. Frozen tile features feed a gated attention-MIL head (Ilse et al., 2018), trained and scored over 10 patient-disjoint folds on subsets of 1,503 TCGA slides, 1,438 of which TinyPath trained on. The paper ran its LongNet slide encoder (Ding et al., 2023) with random splits, so its numbers are not a matched comparison and are left out. Instead, Prov-GigaPath’s tile features go through the same head and folds as TinyPath’s (Table 8).

PORPOISE. The histology-only survival model of Chen et al. (2022), retrained on TCGA-LUAD with the authors’ released labels and five folds and scored by Harrell’s c-index (Harrell et al., 1982). We rebuilt it as closely as the public slides allow, with every tile of a patient’s slides in one bag, but the rebuild departs from the paper’s protocol in three ways: 9 of its 453 patients are missing, tiles come from our own tissue detection and tiling rather than the paper’s, and each fold trains five head seeds where the paper trained one. The published score reflects none of these, so we also run the paper’s own ResNet-50 feature extractor and Prov-GigaPath through the same pipeline; the ResNet-50 run lands inside the published 95% interval (Table 7). (A fourth difference is slight: in these runs the survival loss floors predicted probabilities at float32’s machine epsilon, about \(1.19\times10^{-7}\), rather than at the released code’s \(10^{-7}\), which changes the loss only for probabilities below that floor. Our code now matches; these runs were not retrained.)

H0-mini. Scored through its 768-dimensional class token, as TinyPath is. Its paper scores HEST with the class token concatenated to the mean patch feature, so the class-token leaderboard entries used here, 0.3958 on HEST and 0.541 on PLISM, are not the numbers in its paper. Its published EVA scores do come from the paper, which has no BreakHis or Gleason score and ran EVA before the benchmark’s current settings, so we also report our own EVA run of it. It was distilled for 105,000 steps at batch 2,048 on 43 million tiles from the same TCGA slides as TinyPath, about 215 million tiles seen in all.

Notation

The teacher \(T\) has depth 40 and width \(C_{t}=1536\); the student \(S\) has depth 33 and width \(C_{s}=640\). Both turn a \(224\times224\) tile into \(P=196\) patch tokens plus one CLS token. \(a_{b,n,c}\) is a hooked activation at calibration example \(b\), token \(n\) and component \(c\), and \(\mathrm{LN}\) is a non-affine layer normalization applied token-wise.

The teacher and the student

The teacher is the published Prov-GigaPath tile encoder at a pinned revision, frozen throughout. The student inherits its patch size, token count and head width; every other dimension comes from the architecture search, with the head count tied to width / 64 (Pruning).

The recorded mlp_ratio of 2.4 refers to the packed first projection, which is \(2\times768=1536\) wide because SwiGLU emits a gate branch and a value branch before they are combined; 1,536 over a width of 640 is 2.4. The MLP hidden size is 768, counted after the two branches are combined. That is also where the pruning ranks its dimensions.

Appendix D: Where we depart from the papers Table 17

TinyPath’s recipe borrows from Minitron, DINOv2 and H0-mini without reproducing any of them.

These are tools for building the student, not evidence about it: CKA is not biological performance, and moving EMA targets make the objective no fixed yardstick. The evidence is the separate evaluation, built mostly on published benchmarks, though not on entirely separate data: the results flag each benchmark whose slides TinyPath trained on.
